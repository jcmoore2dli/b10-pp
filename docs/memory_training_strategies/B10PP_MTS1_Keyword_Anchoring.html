Keyword Anchoring

Pick 2–3 words while you listen. Use them to pull back everything else.
What this strategy does: Instead of trying to remember everything at once, you identify 2–3 content-rich words during listening and use them as anchors to retrieve the full meaning when you record.
1
Before you listen
Get ready to catch words, not sentences.

Tell yourself: "I am not going to remember everything. I am going to pick the 2–3 most important words and hold onto those." This is a deliberate shift — most people try to memorize the whole passage and lose it. You are going to anchor instead.

2
While you listen
Identify the 2–3 words that carry the most meaning.

Listen for words that are content-dense — nouns, verbs, or specific terms that the whole passage seems to be about. These are your anchors. You do not need to remember everything around them yet — just lock those words in.

Example
Passage about urban infrastructure and water access: your anchors might be infrastructure, access, and municipal. Not full sentences — just those three words, held clearly.
3
Right after listening
Say your anchor words silently — once.

The moment the passage ends, say your 2–3 anchor words to yourself. Do not try to recall the whole passage yet. Just confirm: do I have my anchors? If one is fuzzy, replace it now with a word you remember more clearly.

4
Before you record
Use each anchor word to pull back what surrounds it.

Take your first anchor word and ask: What was happening around this word in the passage? Let the details come back to you through the anchor, not independently. Do the same for your second and third anchor words. You now have the structure of your response.

Example
Anchor: infrastructure → "The passage said that aging infrastructure is the main barrier to…" Anchor: access → "…to water access in low-income districts." Anchor: municipal → "Municipal governments were identified as responsible for funding the repairs."
5
When you record
Speak through your anchors in order.

Start with the main idea — which anchor word is most central? Build your response from there, using each anchor as a stepping stone. If you lose your place, return to the next anchor word and continue. Do not stop — keep speaking.

Watch out for this
Choosing anchors that are too general — words like policy, people, or problem — will not help you retrieve specific content. Your anchors need to be specific enough that when you say the word, the details come back with it.
Remember: Keyword Anchoring works best when the passage has clear content nouns — specific topics, places, groups, or processes. If you find that your anchors are not pulling back enough detail, try switching to Chunking + Immediate Rehearsal, which builds retention differently, or Gist-First, which prioritizes the main idea before details.
