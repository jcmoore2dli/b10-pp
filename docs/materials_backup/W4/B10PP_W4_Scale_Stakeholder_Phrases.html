Q1 Should AI teachers replace human instructors in some schools?
EDU ▼
Stakeholder
students — individual level
teachers — individual/organizational level
schools and ed-tech companies — organizational level
national education systems and workforce — national level
Impact
personalized pacing; social-emotional development loss
job displacement; role redefinition toward facilitation
infrastructure costs replace salary costs; market expansion
potential reduction of teacher shortage; equity of AI quality risk
Scale Consideration
at the individual student level, outcome varies dramatically — personalization benefits some while social-emotional development loss harms others
at the teacher level, the profession faces structural redefinition — augmentation and replacement have very different individual implications
at the organizational level, cost structure shifts from labor to infrastructure — the equity of that shift depends on who controls the AI
at the national level, the teacher shortage argument is most compelling in under-resourced regions — AI replacement is defensible where the alternative is no teacher, not where it replaces human teaching
scale reveals the equity problem — AI quality inequality at the organizational level becomes educational outcome inequality at the national level
Q2 Should personalized learning algorithms be mandatory in public education?
EDU ▼
Stakeholder
students — individual level
students with learning differences — individual level
teachers — organizational level
ed-tech vendors — organizational level
national education systems and data governance — national level
Impact
adaptive content; privacy exposure; potential under-challenge for advanced students
potential benefit from tailored content delivery
role shift from instructor to facilitator; potential deskilling
guaranteed market if mandated; vendor lock-in risk
student data at scale creates systemic privacy risk; learning outcome variation
Scale Consideration
at the individual level, struggling students may benefit while advanced students may be under-challenged — the policy is not uniformly beneficial
at the teacher level, the role shift is structural — meaningful override authority determines whether this is augmentation or replacement
at the organizational level, vendor dependency is the most serious risk — mandatory adoption without competitive procurement creates lock-in
at the national level, student data at scale is a systemic privacy risk that no current framework fully addresses
scale transforms individual benefit into systemic risk — what helps one student is manageable; what collects data on all students is a national governance challenge
Q3 Should cryptocurrency be regulated as a financial instrument?
ECN ▼
Stakeholder
individual retail investors — individual level
unbanked populations — individual level
crypto exchanges and businesses — organizational level
traditional financial institutions — organizational level
national monetary systems and global financial stability — national/global level
Impact
greater consumer protection; narrowed access if smaller platforms exit
potential financial inclusion tool; risk of regulatory exclusion
compliance costs; market consolidation around compliant large players
competitive pressure; playing field leveling
financial stability risk if unregulated at scale; tax revenue implications
Scale Consideration
at the individual level, retail investor protection is the strongest argument — unsophisticated investors bear disproportionate risk in unregulated markets
at the individual level, unbanked populations face a genuine access trade-off — regulation that protects investors may exclude those who need alternative finance most
at the organizational level, compliance costs consolidate the market — large compliant exchanges win; small innovative ones exit
at the national level, financial stability and tax compliance arguments dominate — individual access concerns do not outweigh systemic risk at scale
scale matters because individual financial inclusion arguments and national financial stability arguments point in different directions — the policy must resolve that tension explicitly
Q4 Should central banks adopt digital currencies?
ECN ▼
Stakeholder
individual citizens — individual level
unbanked populations — individual level
commercial banks — organizational level
payment processors — organizational level
national monetary systems and governments — national level
Impact
financial surveillance concerns; convenience of digital payment
financial inclusion without traditional banking infrastructure
disintermediation risk if citizens hold CBDC directly
significant disruption to existing payment infrastructure
greater monetary policy precision; surveillance capacity increase
Scale Consideration
at the individual level, the surveillance concern and the inclusion benefit are both real — they fall on different populations
at the individual level, unbanked populations stand to gain most — CBDC provides access without the barriers of traditional banking
at the organizational level, commercial banks face a structural threat — disintermediation is not a side effect but a central feature of direct CBDC
at the national level, the monetary policy precision argument is strongest — but so is the government surveillance capacity argument
scale reveals the governance challenge — individual inclusion benefit and national surveillance risk both become more visible at larger scale
Q5 Should governments implement a digital identity system for all citizens?
GOV ▼
Stakeholder
individual citizens — individual level
marginalized groups without documentation — individual level
government agencies — organizational level
technology contractors — organizational level
national state capacity and civil liberties frameworks — national level
Impact
streamlined service access; surveillance and exclusion risks
risk of being excluded from enrollment and therefore from services
improved service delivery and fraud reduction
significant implementation contracts
stronger state capacity; surveillance infrastructure expansion
Scale Consideration
at the individual level, enrolled citizens gain service access while undocumented individuals face deeper exclusion — the policy has opposite effects on different populations
at the individual level, the privacy concern is biometric — the data cannot be changed if compromised, making the risk qualitatively different from other data
at the organizational level, government agencies gain efficiency but surveillance infrastructure is built alongside it — the two are not separable
at the national level, the state capacity argument is strongest for service delivery; the civil liberties argument is strongest for surveillance risk
scale matters because the surveillance infrastructure built at the national level serves purposes beyond the individual service delivery benefit that justified it
Q6 Should governments fund research into life extension technologies?
HLT ▼
Stakeholder
individuals with terminal or age-related illness — individual level
families — individual level
pension and retirement systems — organizational level
research institutions — organizational level
global population dynamics and inequality — national/global level
Impact
potential benefit from extended healthy life
reduced grief and caregiving burden
structural insolvency if life expectancy increases significantly
significant funding and research opportunities
global inequality deepens if access is limited to wealthy nations
Scale Consideration
at the individual level, the suffering reduction argument is compelling — age-related disease causes real and preventable harm
at the family level, the benefit is relational — extended healthy years reduce caregiver burden
at the organizational level, pension systems face structural insolvency if lifespans extend without reform — the benefit creates a simultaneous institutional crisis
at the global level, the equity argument is strongest — if only wealthy nations access life extension, it becomes the ultimate marker of global inequality
scale reveals the distribution problem — individual benefit is clear; global equity risk is equally clear — the two must be addressed together
Q7 Should there be a global moratorium on autonomous weapons development?
TEC ▼
Stakeholder
soldiers — individual level
civilians in conflict zones — individual level
defense contractors — organizational level
military institutions — organizational level
international security system and non-signatory nations — national/global level
Impact
reduced human casualties if autonomous systems replace combatants; accountability disappears
risk of algorithmic targeting errors without human oversight
significant revenue at stake; opposition to moratorium likely
doctrine and command structure disrupted by autonomous systems
arms race dynamics if moratorium fails; precedent for AI weapons governance
Scale Consideration
at the individual soldier level, autonomous weapons reduce human risk — but shift accountability away from human decision-makers
at the individual civilian level, algorithmic targeting errors create a new category of harm without clear legal recourse
at the organizational level, defense contractor interests are directly threatened — their opposition is structural, not incidental
at the national level, non-signatory nations gain asymmetric advantage — the moratorium only works if adoption is broad
scale is the defining challenge — individual protection benefits at the soldier level become collective security risks at the global level if compliance is partial
Q8 Should there be a global ban on nuclear weapons?
INT ▼
Stakeholder
civilian populations near nuclear sites — individual level
citizens of nuclear states — individual level
defense and nuclear industries — organizational level
verification agencies — organizational level
non-nuclear signatory nations — national level
global security architecture — global level
Impact
reduced long-term radiation and accident risk
loss of deterrence protection if ban is uneven
significant economic disruption from disarmament
massive expansion of monitoring responsibilities
asymmetric vulnerability if nuclear states do not comply
existential risk reduction if universal; destabilization if partial
Scale Consideration
at the individual level, the risk reduction benefit is real — accident and miscalculation risk decreases with fewer weapons
at the individual level, deterrence loss is also real — citizens of nuclear states lose protection they currently have
at the organizational level, verification is the binding constraint — the IAEA expansion required is significant
at the national level, non-nuclear signatories face the greatest risk if nuclear states do not comply — they lose deterrence protection without gaining safety
scale is the central challenge — the ban is beneficial at the global level only if compliance is universal; partial adoption creates asymmetric harm at the national level

How to Use These Phrases

Tap a question to expand its three columns. Here is what each column is asking you to do:

Stakeholder = Who Name the specific group affected and the scale level they operate at. "People" and "society" are not stakeholders — name the group precisely.
Impact = What Happens State the specific consequence for that stakeholder — what concretely changes for them as a result of the policy.
Scale Consideration = Why Scale Matters Explain why operating at that particular level changes the argument — what is true at the individual level may be false at the national level, and vice versa.

Pick one chunk from each column and connect them. You must engage at least two scale levels. A response that stays at one level does not demonstrate the analytical depth this frame requires.
