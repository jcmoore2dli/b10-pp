Q1 Should higher education be made tuition-free at public universities?
EDU ▼
Tensions
access and equity benefit vs. fiscal cost and credential inflation risk
government funding replaces tuition vs. quality maintenance without tuition revenue
benefit to all income groups vs. disproportionate benefit to those already likely to attend
long-term workforce and economic benefit vs. short-term fiscal burden
Priorities
access benefit is real but not universal — targeting matters, and free tuition is an untargeted subsidy
quality maintenance is a design challenge, not an inherent obstacle — ring-fenced funding addresses it
disproportionate benefit to higher-income groups is the strongest objection — income-contingent loans may be more targeted
long-term benefit is documented in comparable systems; short-term cost is a political challenge, not an economic objection
Judgment
on balance, free tuition is defensible but not obviously superior to well-designed income-contingent loans — the access argument depends on targeting quality
the stronger case acknowledges credential inflation risk while noting it is addressable through employer engagement and alternative credential development
the defensible position on distributional concern is to combine free tuition with strong need-based support for living costs — tuition is not the only barrier
the judgment on long-term benefit favors free tuition in systems where loan burden has demonstrably suppressed access — the evidence is context-dependent
Q2 Should gig economy workers receive the same benefits as full-time employees?
WRK ▼
Tensions
worker protection vs. flexibility that workers value
platform cost absorption vs. market exit risk
legal reclassification vs. new intermediate employment category
immediate protection benefit vs. long-term gig work availability
Priorities
flexibility argument is strongest for high-income contractors; it does not apply to low-income gig workers who have no real choice
platform cost absorption is feasible for large platforms; market exit risk is real but concentrated among smaller operators
new intermediate category is the most technically elegant solution but the most politically difficult to design and legislate
immediate protection benefit is strongest for lowest-income workers; long-term availability concern is real but overstated by platforms
Judgment
on balance, reclassification with income threshold is defensible — protecting the most vulnerable workers without eliminating flexibility for those who genuinely choose it
the stronger case distinguishes between high-income contractors who choose gig work and low-income workers who have no alternative — the policy should track that distinction
the defensible position on new intermediate category is that it is worth pursuing as a long-term goal while reclassification addresses immediate harm
the judgment on long-term availability is that worker protection and gig work can coexist — platforms that exit are those whose business models depend on labor cost externalization
Q3 Should governments implement universal basic income?
ECN ▼
Tensions
guaranteed income floor vs. work disincentive risk
simplification of welfare system vs. elimination of targeted support for high-need groups
automation buffer vs. fiscal cost at meaningful scale
entrepreneurial freedom benefit vs. inflationary risk if money-creation funded
Priorities
work disincentive risk is real at the margin but smaller than critics claim — evidence from pilots shows modest labor supply effects
simplification benefit is genuine but replacement of targeted programs is the wrong design — supplement, don't replace
automation buffer argument is forward-looking and speculative; fiscal cost is immediate and certain — the timing asymmetry matters
entrepreneurial freedom benefit is real and undervalued in policy debate; inflationary risk is a design question, not an inherent feature
Judgment
on balance, a subsistence-level UBI that supplements rather than replaces targeted programs is defensible — the floor argument is strong and the work disincentive risk is manageable
the stronger case frames UBI as an automation buffer and entrepreneurial enabler, not a welfare replacement — it reframes the political coalition
the defensible position on fiscal cost is phased implementation with evidence review — the uncertainty about automation timing justifies caution about scale
the judgment on inflation risk is that tax-funded UBI avoids it — the design question is not whether UBI causes inflation but how it is financed
Q4 Should social media companies be required to remove political misinformation?
GOV ▼
Tensions
democratic harm of misinformation vs. censorship risk of government-mandated removal
platform accountability vs. platform as private entity with right to set own policies
scale of misinformation harm vs. scale of over-removal harm
uniform enforcement vs. political bias in enforcement
Priorities
democratic harm is real and documented; censorship risk is real but addressable through independent oversight rather than government direct mandate
platform accountability argument is strongest for demonstrably false factual claims; it weakens significantly for contested political opinions
scale comparison favors addressing misinformation — but over-removal of legitimate speech is a cost that must be explicitly weighted in design
political bias in enforcement is the strongest objection — independent oversight with diverse composition addresses it better than government mandate
Judgment
on balance, independent oversight with binding standards is more defensible than direct government mandate — it addresses the censorship risk while maintaining accountability
the stronger case limits removal obligation to demonstrably false factual claims and excludes contested political opinion — scope is the critical design variable
the defensible position on over-removal is that appeals processes and transparency reports reduce the harm — imperfect enforcement of a narrow standard beats no standard
the judgment on political bias is that independent oversight is necessary but not sufficient — composition, transparency, and appeal rights determine whether independence is real
Q5 Should alternative medicine be covered by public health insurance?
HLT ▼
Tensions
patient autonomy and cultural relevance vs. evidence-based resource allocation
access expansion vs. displacement of conventional care investment
coverage of some alternatives vs. blanket coverage regardless of evidence
patient satisfaction benefit vs. delayed diagnosis risk
Priorities
patient autonomy is a genuine value; it does not require public subsidy — the autonomy argument supports the right to choose, not the obligation to fund
displacement risk is real if coverage is unlimited; it is manageable if coverage is tiered by evidence level
tiered coverage based on evidence level is the most defensible design — it honors patient choice without abandoning evidence standards
patient satisfaction benefit is real and improves adherence; delayed diagnosis risk is real and addressable through disclosure requirements
Judgment
on balance, limited evidence-tiered coverage is defensible — blanket coverage is not; the design determines whether the policy is defensible
the stronger case for coverage is made on adherence and cultural relevance grounds, not on efficacy equivalence — the argument is strongest when evidence is clearest
the defensible position is narrow, well-defined coverage with disclosure requirements — broad coverage without evidence threshold is indefensible
the judgment on delayed diagnosis risk is that disclosure requirements and practitioner licensing address it — coverage without those safeguards is not defensible
Q6 Should governments regulate the development of artificial intelligence?
TEC ▼
Tensions
harm prevention vs. innovation cost
domestic regulation vs. competitive disadvantage without international coordination
prescriptive rules vs. principles-based oversight
current harm prevention vs. future risk governance
Priorities
harm prevention argument is strongest for high-risk AI systems with demonstrated harm potential; innovation cost concern is strongest for low-risk systems — risk-tiering addresses both
competitive disadvantage is real without coordination; coordination is achievable through bilateral and multilateral agreements — it is a political challenge, not an inherent obstacle
principles-based oversight is more durable than prescriptive rules in a fast-moving technology environment — it allows adaptation without legislative revision
current harm is addressable now; future risk governance requires regulatory architecture that can adapt — both are necessary and compatible
Judgment
on balance, risk-tiered principles-based regulation with international coordination is defensible — it addresses the innovation cost concern while maintaining the harm prevention imperative
the stronger case leads with documented current harms — algorithmic discrimination, deepfakes, autonomous weapons — rather than speculative future risk
the defensible position on international coordination is to build it through bilateral agreements while maintaining domestic standards — waiting for global consensus means waiting indefinitely
the judgment on prescriptive vs. principles-based is that principles-based with mandatory outcomes is the most durable approach — how is flexible, what is not
Q7 Should governments ban the development of new fossil fuel infrastructure?
ENV ▼
Tensions
climate imperative vs. energy security during transition
carbon lock-in prevention vs. stranded asset cost to investors and workers
domestic ban vs. carbon leakage to unregulated markets
just transition obligation vs. political feasibility in fossil fuel-dependent regions
Priorities
climate imperative is strongest — carbon lock-in from new infrastructure makes 1.5C targets structurally unachievable, energy security is a transition management challenge, not a permanent objection
stranded asset cost is real and concentrated — just transition support addresses the human cost; investor stranded assets are a foreseeable risk in a carbon-constrained economy
carbon leakage is the strongest technical objection — it is addressable through border carbon adjustment but not through domestic ban alone
just transition obligation is non-negotiable and political feasibility follows from it — bans without transition support are politically self-defeating
Judgment
on balance, a ban with robust just transition support and border carbon adjustment is defensible — the climate imperative is the strongest argument and the transition concerns are addressable
the stronger case leads with the carbon lock-in argument — new infrastructure built today operates for 30-50 years, making it structurally incompatible with climate commitments
the defensible position on leakage is that border adjustment is necessary alongside the ban — a ban without border protection is environmentally incomplete
the judgment on political feasibility is that just transition support is the necessary condition — without it, the ban is correct in principle and undeliverable in practice
Q8 Should governments implement stricter gun control measures?
JUS ▼
Tensions
public safety benefit vs. constitutional right restriction
legal firearm access reduction vs. illegal firearm market expansion
mass casualty event lethality reduction vs. limited effect on overall gun homicide rate
urban safety benefit vs. rural compliance burden
Priorities
public safety benefit is documented for specific measures — high-capacity magazine restrictions and background check expansion have clearest evidence; blanket arguments are weaker
illegal market expansion is real but does not outweigh legal access reduction — the marginal deterrent effect of legal availability matters even if illegal markets persist
mass casualty lethality reduction is the strongest evidence-based argument for specific restrictions; overall homicide rate reduction requires addressing social determinants
rural compliance burden is real and legitimate — measure design should account for context differences rather than applying uniform urban-designed rules
Judgment
on balance, targeted measures with clearest evidence — background checks, high-capacity restrictions — are defensible; blanket measures without evidence base are not
the stronger case leads with mass casualty lethality reduction — it has the clearest evidence and the strongest moral claim
the defensible position on illegal markets is that reducing legal supply still matters at the margin — the argument that illegal markets negate legal restrictions proves too much
the judgment on rural burden is that context-sensitive design is achievable and necessary — rural exemptions for specific categories are defensible within a broader framework
