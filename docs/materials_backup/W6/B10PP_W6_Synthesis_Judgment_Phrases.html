Q1Should vocational education be prioritized over academic preparation?
EDU▼
Tensions
documented skilled trades shortage vs. long-term value of academic preparation
risk of tracking lower-income students vs. need to address labor market gaps
employer co-investment aligns training vs. risk of narrowly job-specific curriculum
cultural prestige of academic credentials vs. economic reality of skilled trades demand
Priorities
workforce demand is immediate and structural; academic prestige is a cultural bias that policy can correct
tracking risk is addressable through transfer rights; labor market gap is not addressable without structural change
employer partnership is valuable but must be constrained to prevent curriculum capture by short-term market needs
prestige is a cultural construct that policy has successfully changed in comparable economies
Judgment
on balance, elevating vocational pathways is defensible — provided academic routes remain equally funded
the stronger case is for integration, not prioritization — both pathways deserve equal investment and prestige
a defensible model combines public funding, employer partnership, and transfer rights
the German dual system demonstrates vocational and academic prestige can coexist — that is the more defensible model
Q2Should the electoral college be abolished?
GOV▼
Tensions
democratic legitimacy requires popular vote winner wins vs. federalism requires small state protection
constitutional amendment is practically impossible vs. normative case for abolition is strong
campaign focus on swing states vs. risk of shifting entirely to large urban centers
founders' design as deliberate check vs. founders' design reflected slavery-era compromises
Priorities
small state protection already exists through Senate representation — it does not require the presidency as well
the practical barrier does not strengthen the normative case — it only constrains the reform path
swing state problem affects few states; urban concentration affects many voters currently without meaningful voice
not all founding-era design choices deserve preservation; the slavery compromise does not strengthen the federalism argument
Judgment
on balance, the democratic legitimacy argument is stronger — three popular vote losers as president is a legitimacy deficit federalism cannot offset
the realistic path is the National Popular Vote Interstate Compact — imperfect but achievable without the amendment threshold
net geographic equity favors popular vote — more voters are currently disenfranchised than would be under a popular vote
original intent is not sufficient to maintain a system whose legitimacy deficits are now well-documented
Q3Should judges be elected or appointed?
GOV▼
Tensions
judicial accountability to citizens vs. judicial independence from political pressure
appointed judges reflect ideology of appointing authority vs. elected judges reflect donor preferences
minority rights protection vs. democratic responsiveness of judicial decision-making
merit-based appointment produces better judges vs. elections make judicial philosophy more transparent
Priorities
independence is more foundational — a judge who fears electoral consequence cannot adjudicate impartially
donor influence in elections is more directly transactional than ideological alignment in appointment
minority rights are most at risk when majoritarian sentiment is strongest — when elected judges face the most pressure
transparency can be achieved through confirmation hearings and published opinions without electoral exposure
Judgment
on balance, appointment with retention review is more defensible — accountability without continuous political exposure
appointment is the lesser distortion — ideological alignment is less corrosive than donor dependency
the stronger case for appointment rests on minority rights — the judiciary's most important function is least compatible with electoral accountability
transparency does not require elections — it requires a rigorous, public appointment process
Q4Should education systems prioritize critical thinking over content knowledge?
EDU▼
Tensions
knowledge economy demands critical thinking vs. critical thinking requires content knowledge as its foundation
content knowledge becomes obsolete quickly vs. critical thinking skills are durable
assessment systems measure content recall, not reasoning vs. assessment reform is politically difficult
employer demand for critical thinking vs. university admissions based on content mastery
Priorities
the opposition is false — you cannot think critically about nothing; depth of content enables depth of analysis
durability of critical thinking does not make content dispensable — it means content selected for depth rather than coverage
assessment drives curriculum — curriculum reform without assessment reform produces compliance without change
employer signal is more diagnostic of graduate outcomes, which is what education is ultimately for
Judgment
on balance, the stronger case is for integration — the framing of the question is itself a false dichotomy
the defensible curriculum reduces content breadth to enable critical engagement depth — less content, thought more carefully
curriculum and assessment reform must be pursued together — one without the other produces the worst of both
align curriculum with long-term graduate outcomes rather than short-term institutional gatekeeping requirements
Q5Should there be stricter regulations on the use of cultural appropriation in art?
CUL▼
Tensions
source community rights vs. artistic freedom to engage with other cultures
no legal definition of appropriation exists vs. harm from commercial exploitation is documented
source community self-determination vs. risk of government becoming cultural gatekeeper
chilling effect on cross-cultural exchange vs. ongoing commercial harm to source communities
Priorities
commercial exploitation without consent is meaningfully different from artistic borrowing — the law can distinguish between them
definitional difficulty does not eliminate the obligation to try — IP law manages similar complexity
vesting legal standing in source communities rather than government bodies addresses the gatekeeping risk directly
a narrow commercial scope minimizes chilling effect while addressing the primary harm
Judgment
on balance, regulations targeting commercial exploitation are defensible — scope matters as much as principle
the stronger case is for a narrow, well-defined framework targeting commercial exploitation rather than broad prohibition
the defensible model gives communities legal standing to pursue their own claims — not a government body determining what counts as their culture
broadly drawn regulations are indefensible; narrowly drawn commercial exploitation frameworks are defensible and necessary
Q6Should governments provide universal healthcare as an economic right?
ECN▼
Tensions
healthcare as a human right vs. healthcare as a service best delivered by markets
fiscal cost of universal coverage vs. cost of medical bankruptcy under current system
innovation incentive requires market pricing vs. universal access requires cost control
transition disruption from existing system vs. long-term benefit of universal coverage
Priorities
every high-income country except one concluded universal access produces better outcomes at lower cost — evidence favors the rights framework
the current system's costs are distributed differently, not eliminated — universal systems reduce administrative inefficiency and uncompensated care
pharmaceutical innovation occurs across universal systems — the trade-off is overstated by industry
transition cost is a one-time political challenge; access inequality under the current system is permanent and ongoing
Judgment
on balance, the case for universal healthcare as a right is stronger — market failure in healthcare is well-documented and international comparison is dispositive
the stronger fiscal argument is for universal coverage — the question is not whether we pay, but how efficiently and equitably
the innovation argument does not outweigh the access argument — universal systems can maintain incentives through regulated pricing
a phased transition is the defensible path — the argument against transition is an argument for delay, not against the destination
Q7Should tech companies be required to make their algorithms transparent?
TEC▼
Tensions
public accountability for high-stakes algorithms vs. trade secret protection for proprietary systems
full transparency enables gaming vs. opacity enables unaccountable discrimination
market competition as accountability mechanism vs. regulatory oversight as accountability mechanism
technical complexity makes transparency meaningless vs. right to explanation is fundamental regardless of complexity
Priorities
trade secret protection cannot shield systems making consequential decisions from any form of accountability
unaccountable discrimination is more harmful than gaming risk — gaming can be managed through system design
market competition does not produce accountability when harm is invisible — users cannot choose against what they cannot see
meaningful explanation is achievable without full technical disclosure — the right is to understand the basis of a decision, not audit the entire system
Judgment
on balance, third-party audit with confidentiality protections is more defensible than either full disclosure or no disclosure
the stronger case is that unaccountable discrimination is more harmful — discrimination without accountability cannot be managed
the market accountability argument fails for algorithmic systems — regulatory oversight is necessary because market signals are insufficient
the defensible requirement is decision-level explanation for affected individuals — targeted, meaningful, and achievable
Q8Should governments implement a carbon border tax on imports?
ENV▼
Tensions
preventing carbon leakage vs. imposing trade barriers on developing nations
WTO compatibility is uncertain vs. climate obligation requires action despite legal risk
incentive for trading partners to adopt carbon pricing vs. risk of retaliatory tariffs
administrative complexity of calculating embodied carbon vs. imprecision undermines the policy
Priorities
carbon leakage renders domestic climate policy ineffective — but developing nations should not bear costs without support
legal risk is manageable through careful design — the EU CBAM demonstrates WTO-compatible design is achievable
the incentive effect is documented — carbon border taxes have already prompted trading partners to accelerate domestic carbon pricing
perfect precision is not required — default values based on sector averages provide sufficient incentive signal
Judgment
on balance, a carbon border tax with grace periods and capacity support for developing nations is defensible — leakage is real and equity concerns are addressable through design
the legal argument does not outweigh the climate argument — pursue WTO-compatible design rather than abandon the policy on legal uncertainty
the incentive argument strengthens the case — it is both a domestic climate protection measure and an international climate policy lever
imperfect implementation of a sound policy is preferable to no policy in the face of documented carbon leakage

How to Use These Phrases

Tap a question to expand its three columns. Phrases are aligned — T1 pairs with P1 pairs with J1. Pick one number across all three columns to build a complete argument chain. For example, using row 1 from Q2:

"While both the democratic legitimacy and federalism arguments have merit, the stronger case rests on legitimacy — small state protection already exists through Senate representation and does not require the presidency as well. On balance, a system that has produced three popular vote losers as president has a legitimacy deficit that federalism concerns cannot offset."

Three chunks, one number, one complete argument. The Priorities column is where ILR 3 performance lives — not identifying the tension, but explaining why one side carries more weight than the other.
