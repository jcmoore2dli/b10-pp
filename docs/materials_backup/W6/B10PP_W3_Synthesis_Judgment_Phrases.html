Q1 Should schools integrate media literacy education into their curricula?
EDU ▼
Tensions
civic benefit of informed citizenry vs. curriculum crowding with existing subject demands
teacher capacity to deliver vs. cost and time of training
standalone course vs. integrated cross-subject approach
short-term implementation cost vs. long-term democratic dividend
Priorities
civic benefit is real and documented; curriculum crowding is addressable through integration rather than addition
teacher capacity is the binding constraint — investment in training is the necessary precondition for the benefit to materialize
integrated cross-subject approach is more durable and less costly than standalone — it avoids the addition problem while distributing delivery across trained educators
long-term democratic dividend justifies short-term cost — the question is whether political time horizons allow for that calculus
Judgment
on balance, integrated cross-subject media literacy is defensible — it addresses the crowding objection while distributing the benefit across all subjects
the stronger case leads with the democratic dividend argument for general audiences and the cost-effectiveness of integration for institutional audiences
the defensible position on teacher capacity is that it must be addressed first — mandate without training produces compliance without benefit
the judgment on political time horizon is that the democratic dividend argument is strongest when made in terms of near-term harms — misinformation spread is current, not future
Q2 Should the gig economy be more strictly regulated?
WRK ▼
Tensions
worker protection benefit vs. gig work availability reduction
platform accountability vs. platform market exit risk
uniform national regulation vs. sector-specific or income-threshold approaches
immediate benefit to low-income workers vs. long-term structural labor market effects
Priorities
worker protection benefit is strongest for low-income workers with no real choice; availability reduction risk is strongest for high-income contractors who do choose gig work
platform accountability is appropriate; market exit risk is real for smaller platforms but overstated for large ones with pricing power to absorb costs
income-threshold approach is more defensible than uniform regulation — it protects the most vulnerable without restricting genuine independent contracting
immediate benefit to low-income workers is the strongest moral claim; long-term structural effects are uncertain and should not override the immediate claim
Judgment
on balance, income-threshold reclassification is defensible — it targets the most vulnerable workers while preserving flexibility for genuine contractors
the stronger case distinguishes platform types — large platforms with documented exploitation histories are the primary target; smaller platforms and genuine contracting relationships are a secondary concern
the defensible position on market exit is that platforms whose business models depend on labor cost externalization should restructure or exit — that is the point of the regulation
the judgment on long-term effects is that uncertainty about structural effects does not override certainty about current harm — the moral claim of the most vulnerable workers is the strongest argument
Q3 Should there be stronger antitrust laws to break up monopolies?
ECN ▼
Tensions
consumer and innovation benefit vs. scale efficiency disruption
structural remedies vs. behavioral remedies
domestic antitrust vs. global competitive disadvantage
speed of antitrust litigation vs. speed of market harm
Priorities
scale efficiency argument is strongest for natural monopolies; it is much weaker for digital platform monopolies that achieved scale through network effects and acquisition
structural remedies are more durable than behavioral remedies — behavioral compliance is gameable and monitoring-intensive; structural separation is self-enforcing
global competitive disadvantage argument has merit for export-oriented industries but does not apply to domestic market digital platforms
litigation speed is a genuine problem — the market often consolidates further during litigation; interim remedies and faster process are the appropriate response
Judgment
on balance, stronger antitrust with preference for structural remedies is defensible for digital platform monopolies — the scale efficiency argument does not apply to this category
the stronger case distinguishes natural monopolies from acquired monopolies — the antitrust argument is strongest where market dominance was achieved through acquisition rather than organic growth
the defensible position on global competitiveness is context-dependent — it is a legitimate concern for export industries and not for domestic service platforms
the judgment on litigation speed is that process reform is necessary alongside substantive reform — slower processes than markets makes antitrust structurally reactive
Q4 Should governments implement stricter regulations on lobbying?
GOV ▼
Tensions
reduced corporate influence vs. legitimate stakeholder access restriction
revolving door constraints vs. government capacity to attract industry expertise
disclosure requirements vs. dark money channel persistence
equal application to all lobbyists vs. disproportionate impact on civil society
Priorities
legitimate stakeholder access is valuable; the question is whether current access is proportionate — evidence of regulatory capture suggests it is not
revolving door constraint is the most important reform; government capacity concern is real but addressable through technical advisory roles that do not involve lobbying
disclosure is necessary but not sufficient — dark money channels persist without 501(c)(4) reform alongside lobbying disclosure
equal application is the right principle; disproportionate impact on civil society is a design flaw to be corrected, not an argument against reform
Judgment
on balance, lobbying reform with disclosure, cooling-off periods, and 501(c)(4) reform is defensible — the regulatory capture evidence makes the case for reform compelling
the stronger case leads with the revolving door argument — it is the most concrete and documentable form of regulatory capture
the defensible position on disclosure is that it is necessary but insufficient — the full reform package must include dark money restriction
the judgment on civil society impact is that design can address it — the reform should explicitly protect non-profit advocacy while targeting commercial lobbying
Q5 Should governments implement sugar taxes on processed foods?
HLT ▼
Tensions
public health benefit vs. regressive distributional impact
manufacturer reformulation incentive vs. consumer substitution to equally unhealthy alternatives
revenue generation vs. revenue decline as policy succeeds
individual freedom argument vs. externalized public health cost argument
Priorities
public health benefit is real but concentrated in price-sensitive lower-income groups — which creates both the greatest benefit and the greatest regressive burden simultaneously
reformulation incentive is documented; consumer substitution is real but partial — the net effect on consumption is positive even if substitution occurs
revenue decline as the policy succeeds is a feature, not a bug — it indicates the policy is working; fiscal planning should account for it
externalized public health cost argument is stronger than individual freedom argument — the cost of diet-related disease is borne collectively, not individually
Judgment
on balance, a sugar tax with household rebate mechanism and hypothecated revenue is defensible — the regressive burden is addressable and the public health benefit is real
the stronger case leads with the externalized cost argument — it reframes the policy from government interference in individual choice to correction of a market failure
the defensible position on substitution is that the net effect is positive even with substitution — the policy goal is reduced sugar consumption, not eliminated consumption
the judgment on revenue decline is that successful policy creates fiscal planning requirements — governments should model revenue trajectories and plan accordingly rather than treating declining revenue as a failure
Q6 Should governments regulate the use of social media algorithms?
TEC ▼
Tensions
harm reduction benefit vs. innovation and business model disruption
government content control risk vs. platform unaccountability risk
transparency requirement vs. gaming of disclosed systems
domestic regulation vs. global platform with cross-border effect
Priorities
harm reduction benefit is strongest for documented harms — radicalization, eating disorder content, election interference; innovation disruption concern is strongest for low-harm recommendation systems
government content control risk is addressable through independent oversight; platform unaccountability risk is the current status quo — the comparison favors regulation
gaming risk is real but manageable through third-party audit rather than public disclosure — auditors see the system, adversaries do not
domestic regulation is insufficient alone but necessary — international coordination follows from domestic standard-setting, not precedes it
Judgment
on balance, harm-based algorithmic regulation with third-party audit is defensible — the unaccountability risk of the status quo is stronger than the innovation disruption concern
the stronger case leads with documented harms and their causal mechanisms — radicalization through engagement optimization is the clearest case
the defensible position on transparency is third-party audit with confidentiality — it provides accountability without gaming vulnerability
the judgment on cross-border effect is that domestic standard-setting creates de facto global standards for platforms that want market access — the EU DSA demonstrates this
Q7 Should governments implement a carbon tax on all fossil fuel usage?
ENV ▼
Tensions
emissions reduction effectiveness vs. regressive distributional burden
price signal efficiency vs. political feasibility
revenue recycling options vs. revenue earmarking debates
domestic carbon price vs. carbon leakage without border adjustment
Priorities
emissions reduction effectiveness is documented; regressive burden is real and addressable through carbon dividend — the two are not in fundamental tension
price signal efficiency is the strongest economic argument; political feasibility is a design challenge — carbon dividend addresses the political coalition problem
carbon dividend to households is the most politically durable recycling mechanism — it builds the coalition of net beneficiaries among lower-income households
carbon leakage is the strongest technical objection — border carbon adjustment is necessary alongside domestic pricing to complete the policy
Judgment
on balance, a carbon tax with carbon dividend and border adjustment is defensible — each element addresses one of the three strongest objections
the stronger case leads with the distributional argument for carbon dividend — it reframes carbon tax from regressive burden to progressive transfer
the defensible position on revenue recycling is carbon dividend over green investment earmarking — the coalition benefit of dividend outweighs the investment argument for most political contexts
the judgment on border adjustment is that it is not optional — a carbon tax without border adjustment is environmentally incomplete and economically unfair to domestic industry
Q8 Should there be stricter international regulations on deep-sea fishing?
INT ▼
Tensions
ocean ecosystem protection vs. food security and fishing community livelihoods
binding international regulation vs. voluntary framework and bilateral agreements
commercial fleet restriction vs. artisanal fishing exemption design
short-term catch reduction vs. long-term stock recovery and sustainable yield
Priorities
ecosystem protection and food security are not fundamentally opposed — sustainable stocks produce higher long-term yields than depleted ones
voluntary frameworks have a documented record of underfulfillment; binding regulation with enforcement is more reliable for global commons problems
artisanal fishing exemption is justified by both equity and proportionality — the harm from small-scale fishing is marginal compared to industrial fleet impact
short-term catch reduction is the necessary cost of long-term stock recovery — the time horizon determines whether the trade-off is acceptable
Judgment
on balance, binding international regulation with artisanal exemptions and compensation for affected fishing nations is defensible — the ecosystem and food security arguments align over longer time horizons
the stronger case leads with the long-term food security argument — it reframes conservation from environmentalism to economic self-interest for fishing-dependent nations
the defensible position on voluntary vs. binding is that global commons problems require binding frameworks — the tragedy of the commons is the argument, not an objection
the judgment on time horizon is that shorter stock recovery timelines produce greater political support — framing in terms of 10-year recovery rather than 30-year sustainability increases feasibility
