Q1 Should universities limit legacy admissions?
EDU ▼
Tensions
meritocratic access vs. institutional fundraising dependency
first-generation student benefit vs. upstream preparation inequality persistence
private institutional autonomy vs. social equity obligation
short-term donor revenue loss vs. long-term reputational and mission benefit
Priorities
meritocratic access argument is strong; fundraising dependency is a structural problem that requires solution on its own terms, not through admissions preference
first-generation benefit is real but limited if upstream preparation inequality persists — eliminating legacy is necessary but not sufficient for genuine equity
private institutional autonomy is legitimate; social equity obligation is also legitimate — the question is which is weightier for institutions that receive public subsidy
short-term revenue loss is real; long-term reputational benefit of meritocratic mission alignment is harder to quantify but real
Judgment
on balance, eliminating legacy preference is defensible for institutions receiving public subsidy — the equity obligation outweighs the autonomy argument in that context
the stronger case acknowledges upstream preparation inequality while arguing that eliminating legacy is the necessary first step — necessary conditions can be satisfied sequentially
the defensible position on institutional autonomy is that it does not apply without qualification to institutions receiving public funding and tax exemptions
the judgment on revenue is that alternative fundraising models exist and have worked — the revenue argument does not justify the admissions preference
Q2 Should companies be required to disclose pay scales for all positions?
WRK ▼
Tensions
pay equity benefit vs. internal compensation tension
discriminatory gap reduction vs. high earner retention risk
market efficiency benefit vs. management flexibility loss
evidence of effectiveness vs. cultural resistance to salary transparency
Priorities
pay equity benefit is documented in jurisdictions with pay transparency laws; internal tension is real but manageable through communication and standardization
discriminatory gap reduction is the strongest argument; high earner retention risk is real but limited to a specific segment and does not outweigh the equity benefit
market efficiency benefit is genuine — transparency reduces information asymmetry that currently favors employers; management flexibility loss is modest in well-designed disclosure frameworks
evidence from EU and state-level implementations supports effectiveness; cultural resistance is real but has been overcome in contexts with enforcement
Judgment
on balance, mandatory pay scale disclosure is defensible — the equity benefit is documented and the internal tension is manageable
the stronger case leads with the gender and racial pay gap evidence — it is the most concrete and politically compelling argument
the defensible position on high earner retention is that organizations with large discriminatory gaps are the most vulnerable to retention pressure — a feature, not a bug
the judgment on cultural resistance is that enforcement is the determining factor — disclosure without enforcement produces cosmetic compliance; disclosure with enforcement produces real change
Q3 Should inheritance taxes be significantly increased?
ECN ▼
Tensions
wealth concentration reduction vs. individual property rights
revenue for equal opportunity investment vs. avoidance through trusts and gifting
family business and farm protection vs. tax rate increase
meritocracy argument vs. double taxation argument
Priorities
wealth concentration reduction is documented and significant; property rights argument is legitimate but does not preclude all inheritance taxation — the question is degree
revenue for equal opportunity is compelling; avoidance is real but reducible through simultaneous loophole closure — the avoidance argument is strongest when loopholes remain open
family business and farm protection is a legitimate design requirement; it is addressable through installment payment options and threshold design
meritocracy argument is stronger than double taxation argument — wealth transferred at death is untaxed income to the recipient, not taxed income of the deceased
Judgment
on balance, significantly increased inheritance tax with loophole closure and business protections is defensible — the wealth concentration argument is strong and the practical objections are addressable
the stronger case leads with the meritocracy argument — it is the most resonant framing for audiences skeptical of redistribution
the defensible position on avoidance is that it is a design challenge, not a fundamental objection — closing gift and trust loopholes simultaneously is the necessary complement
the judgment on family business is that installment payment options address the liquidity problem without undermining the tax — the forced sale concern is real but addressable
Q4 Should there be stricter limits on political donations from corporations?
GOV ▼
Tensions
political equality vs. free speech protection for political expenditure
regulatory capture reduction vs. reduced information flow to legislators
corporate donor restriction vs. equal restriction on unions and non-profits
domestic donation limits vs. dark money channel persistence
Priorities
political equality argument is stronger than free speech argument for corporate entities — corporations are not citizens and the constitutional equation of money with speech is contested
regulatory capture reduction is the strongest practical argument; information flow concern is addressable through lobbying disclosure requirements separate from donation limits
equal restriction on all organizational donors is the most defensible design — differential treatment creates asymmetry that undermines the principle
dark money channel persistence is the strongest practical objection — donation limits without 501(c)(4) reform produce substitution rather than reduction
Judgment
on balance, corporate donation limits with equal application and dark money closure is defensible — the political equality argument is strong and the practical objections are addressable through comprehensive design
the stronger case leads with regulatory capture — it is the most concrete and documentable harm from unrestricted corporate political spending
the defensible position on equal application is that it is both principled and strategically necessary — asymmetric restrictions will be challenged and struck down
the judgment on dark money is that donation limits without dark money closure are insufficient — the reform package must be comprehensive or the substitution effect negates the benefit
Q5 Should euthanasia be legalized in all countries?
HLT ▼
Tensions
individual autonomy at end of life vs. coercion risk for vulnerable populations
dignity in dying vs. sanctity of life
medical profession duty of care vs. patient self-determination
safeguard effectiveness vs. eligibility criteria expansion over time
Priorities
individual autonomy argument is strong for competent adults with terminal diagnosis; coercion risk is strongest for elderly, disabled, and economically dependent populations
dignity in dying is a compelling value; sanctity of life is an equally compelling value in different moral traditions — the disagreement is normative, not empirical
medical profession duty of care tension is real; voluntary participation addresses it for individual physicians but does not resolve the institutional tension
safeguard effectiveness evidence is mixed — some jurisdictions show stability, others show eligibility expansion over time
Judgment
on balance, legalization with strict terminal illness criteria and robust safeguards is defensible in jurisdictions where palliative care is well-developed — universal legalization is not defensible given variation in care quality
the stronger case is conditional: autonomy argument is strongest where palliative care alternatives are genuinely available — without them, the choice is not genuinely free
the defensible position on voluntary participation is that it is necessary but not sufficient — institutional culture and economic pressure on the medical profession require structural attention
the judgment on eligibility expansion is that it is a design and governance challenge that must be addressed explicitly — sunset clauses and mandatory review periods are the appropriate response
Q6 Should governments regulate the collection and use of biometric data?
TEC ▼
Tensions
civil liberties protection vs. public safety and security benefit
algorithmic bias harm vs. security effectiveness
surveillance infrastructure constraint vs. criminal investigation capability
comprehensive regulation vs. use-case specific regulation
Priorities
civil liberties protection argument is strong; public safety benefit is real but does not justify unlimited surveillance — proportionality is the governing principle
algorithmic bias harm is documented and falls disproportionately on minority communities; security effectiveness is real but achievable through less biased means
surveillance infrastructure constraint is the strongest argument — infrastructure built for one purpose is historically repurposed for others
use-case specific regulation is more defensible than comprehensive ban — it allows beneficial applications while restricting harmful ones
Judgment
on balance, use-case specific regulation with algorithmic bias audit, consent requirements, and independent oversight is defensible — comprehensive ban and comprehensive permission are both indefensible
the stronger case leads with the algorithmic bias evidence — it is documented, disproportionate, and remediable through audit requirements
the defensible position on surveillance infrastructure is that mission creep must be addressed through structural constraints, not just legal prohibitions — legal prohibitions are revisable, structural constraints are more durable
the judgment on use-case specificity is that high-risk uses — mass surveillance, predictive policing — require stricter standards than low-risk uses — device authentication, border control
Q7 Should private prisons be banned?
JUS ▼
Tensions
justice as public good vs. market efficiency argument
profit motive misalignment vs. government management track record
rural economic dependency vs. rehabilitation investment priority
transition cost and disruption vs. long-term recidivism reduction benefit
Priorities
justice as public good argument is strong; market efficiency argument fails because the outcomes that matter — rehabilitation, recidivism — are not what profit-seeking operators optimize for
profit motive misalignment is the strongest argument; government management track record is mixed but improvable through investment — the comparison is between current private and reformed public, not current private and current public
rural economic dependency is real and requires transition support; rehabilitation investment priority is the more important long-term consideration
transition cost is one-time and manageable; long-term recidivism reduction benefit is ongoing and compounds over time
Judgment
on balance, banning private prisons with robust just transition support is defensible — the profit motive misalignment argument is compelling and the transition concerns are addressable
the stronger case leads with the structural incentive argument — private prisons have a documented financial interest in maintaining high incarceration rates that is incompatible with justice system goals
the defensible position on government track record is that the comparison should be to well-resourced public facilities, not to current underfunded ones — the policy should include investment standards
the judgment on transition is that rural economic impact is the binding political constraint — transition support is not optional, it is the necessary condition for political achievability
Q8 Should there be stricter international regulations on the use of private military companies?
INT ▼
Tensions
accountability gap closure vs. state operational flexibility loss
civilian protection vs. legitimate security gap filling
binding regulation vs. voluntary framework adequacy
PMC industry consolidation risk vs. accountability improvement
Priorities
accountability gap closure is the strongest argument; operational flexibility loss is real but manageable — states retain other instruments of flexible force projection
civilian protection is the foundational argument; legitimate security gap filling is a real function that regulation should accommodate rather than eliminate
voluntary framework inadequacy is documented — the Montreux Document and ICoC have minimal compliance effect; binding regulation with consequences is necessary
industry consolidation is a risk of regulation but not a fatal objection — larger more accountable firms are preferable to smaller less accountable ones
Judgment
on balance, binding international regulation with PMC licensing and state liability is defensible — the accountability gap is real and the voluntary framework has failed
the stronger case leads with civilian harm documentation — it is concrete, credible, and directly attributable to the accountability gap
the defensible position on operational flexibility is that states have sufficient legitimate instruments of flexible power — PMC use for deniable operations is the specific function that regulation should target
the judgment on consolidation is that the direction of travel matters — more accountable consolidated firms are the appropriate outcome of regulation, not the unintended consequence to be avoided
