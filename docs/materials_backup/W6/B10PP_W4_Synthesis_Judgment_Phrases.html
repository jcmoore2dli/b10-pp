Q1 Should AI teachers replace human instructors in some schools?
EDU ▼
Tensions
access expansion in underserved areas vs. social-emotional development loss
teacher shortage solution vs. teaching profession deskilling
cost efficiency vs. equity of AI quality across income groups
personalized learning benefit vs. human mentorship irreplaceability
Priorities
access expansion argument is strongest where teacher shortage is most severe — rural and remote contexts with no alternatives
teaching profession deskilling is a real long-term risk; augmentation framing addresses it better than replacement framing
cost efficiency benefit is real but only defensible if savings are reinvested in student support rather than extracted as profit
personalized learning benefit is context-dependent; human mentorship is most important for students with greatest social-emotional need — often the same students in underserved schools
Judgment
on balance, AI augmentation rather than replacement is defensible in teacher-shortage contexts — replacement in well-resourced schools is not justified
the stronger case is narrow and conditional: AI teaching is defensible where the alternative is no teaching, not where the alternative is human teaching
the defensible position on cost efficiency is that the benefit accrues to students only if savings are reinvested — profit extraction from public education is the primary risk
the judgment on social-emotional development is that it cannot be compensated by personalized content delivery — the most vulnerable students need human presence most
Q2 Should personalized learning algorithms be mandatory in public education?
EDU ▼
Tensions
struggling student benefit vs. advanced student under-challenge risk
evidence of effectiveness vs. vendor interest in mandatory market
data-driven improvement vs. student privacy at scale
teacher facilitation role vs. teacher deskilling risk
Priorities
struggling student benefit is documented for some implementations; advanced student risk is addressable through teacher override authority
evidence of effectiveness is mixed and context-dependent — mandatory adoption without evidence review serves vendor interests more than student interests
data-driven improvement is genuinely valuable; student privacy at scale is a systemic risk that no current framework fully addresses
teacher facilitation role can be enriching rather than deskilling — if teachers retain meaningful authority over algorithmic recommendations
Judgment
on balance, voluntary adoption with evidence review is more defensible than mandatory adoption — the evidence base does not yet support mandate
the stronger case against mandatory adoption is the vendor interest argument — mandatory markets without evidence review are policy capture by industry
the defensible position on privacy is that it is a threshold requirement, not a trade-off — mandatory adoption without adequate privacy protection is not defensible regardless of learning outcomes
the judgment on teacher authority is that meaningful override rights are necessary — algorithms that teachers cannot override are not augmentation tools, they are replacement tools
Q3 Should cryptocurrency be regulated as a financial instrument?
ECN ▼
Tensions
consumer protection benefit vs. innovation restriction cost
money laundering reduction vs. legitimate privacy use restriction
institutional adoption enablement vs. retail investor access reduction
domestic regulation vs. offshore migration of unregulated activity
Priorities
consumer protection benefit is strongest for retail investors with least sophistication — it is least compelling for institutional investors who can protect themselves
money laundering reduction is documented; legitimate privacy use is real but a small proportion of crypto activity — the balance favors regulation
institutional adoption enablement is a genuine benefit of regulatory clarity; retail access reduction is addressable through proportional compliance thresholds
offshore migration is real but partial — regulated platforms in major markets still dominate volume even when unregulated alternatives exist
Judgment
on balance, proportional regulation that protects retail investors while enabling institutional adoption is defensible — blanket restriction or blanket exemption are both indefensible
the stronger case leads with retail investor protection — the most concrete and documented harm from unregulated crypto is to unsophisticated investors
the defensible position on money laundering is that regulation reduces it at the margin even if offshore alternatives persist — marginal deterrence is worth pursuing
the judgment on offshore migration is that major market regulation still shapes global behavior — the EU and US markets are too large for platforms to ignore
Q4 Should central banks adopt digital currencies?
ECN ▼
Tensions
financial inclusion benefit vs. commercial bank disintermediation
monetary policy precision vs. government financial surveillance risk
payment system modernization vs. cybersecurity concentration risk
programmable money benefit vs. government spending control risk
Priorities
financial inclusion benefit is strongest where unbanked populations are largest; commercial bank disintermediation is manageable through hybrid distribution models
monetary policy precision is genuinely valuable; surveillance risk is addressable through privacy-by-design but not eliminable without it
payment system modernization benefit is real; cybersecurity concentration risk is the strongest technical objection — single point of failure is a genuine architectural concern
programmable money benefit is real for specific use cases; spending control risk depends entirely on governance design — authoritarian misuse is a governance failure, not an inherent feature
Judgment
on balance, CBDC with privacy-by-design, hybrid distribution, and strong governance is defensible — each element addresses one of the three strongest objections
the stronger case leads with financial inclusion in high-unbanked contexts — it is the most compelling benefit and the hardest to achieve through other means
the defensible position on surveillance risk is that privacy-by-design is a threshold requirement — CBDC without it is not defensible regardless of other benefits
the judgment on programmable money is that governance design determines the risk — the feature is not inherently dangerous, but weak governance makes it so
Q5 Should governments implement a digital identity system for all citizens?
GOV ▼
Tensions
service delivery efficiency vs. surveillance infrastructure expansion
identity fraud reduction vs. data breach catastrophic risk
financial inclusion for unbanked vs. exclusion of undocumented populations
voluntary vs. mandatory adoption
Priorities
service delivery efficiency is real and documented; surveillance infrastructure expansion is the strongest objection — the same infrastructure serves both purposes and mission creep is historically documented
identity fraud reduction is valuable; catastrophic data breach risk is the strongest technical objection — biometric data cannot be changed if compromised
financial inclusion benefit is strongest for populations currently excluded; exclusion of undocumented populations is a design failure that must be addressed proactively
voluntary adoption with strong incentives is more defensible than mandatory adoption — it allows iterative trust-building and reduces coercion risk
Judgment
on balance, voluntary digital identity with privacy-by-design, independent oversight, and non-digital alternatives is defensible — mandatory adoption without those safeguards is not
the stronger case leads with the service delivery and financial inclusion benefits — they are most compelling for populations currently underserved by analog systems
the defensible position on surveillance risk is that independent oversight with legal limits on data use is necessary but not sufficient — mission creep requires structural constraints, not just legal ones
the judgment on voluntary vs. mandatory is that voluntary adoption with strong incentives achieves near-universal coverage while reducing coercion risk — the difference in coverage rate does not justify mandatory adoption
Q6 Should governments fund research into life extension technologies?
HLT ▼
Tensions
reduction of age-related suffering vs. pension and retirement system insolvency
healthspan extension vs. lifespan extension — different targets with different implications
public good argument for research vs. equity of access to outcomes
opportunity cost vs. long-term economic return from extended productive years
Priorities
age-related suffering reduction is compelling; pension insolvency is a policy design challenge, not an inherent objection — systems can be reformed in parallel
healthspan extension is more defensible than lifespan extension — it reduces suffering and increases productive years without compounding population dynamics
public good argument is strongest for basic research; access equity is the critical condition — research that produces treatments accessible only to wealthy nations is a mixed good at best
opportunity cost is real; long-term economic return is speculative but positive — the question is whether the speculative return justifies the certain cost at current funding levels
Judgment
on balance, publicly funded healthspan extension research with access equity conditions is defensible — lifespan extension without equity conditions is not
the stronger case distinguishes healthspan from lifespan — the suffering reduction argument is strongest for diseases of aging, not for radical life extension
the defensible position on equity is that access conditions should be built into research funding agreements, not added as afterthoughts
the judgment on opportunity cost is that the comparison is with other health research spending — healthspan extension competes favorably with many current research priorities on both suffering reduction and economic return grounds
Q7 Should there be a global moratorium on autonomous weapons development?
TEC ▼
Tensions
civilian protection benefit vs. asymmetric disadvantage for signatory nations
verification feasibility vs. scale of existential risk
existing international law applicability vs. legal gap for autonomous systems
moratorium on all autonomous weapons vs. moratorium on fully autonomous lethal systems only
Priorities
civilian protection benefit is strongest; asymmetric disadvantage is real but manageable through broad multilateral adoption — narrow adoption creates the asymmetry
verification is imperfect but the scale of potential harm from autonomous weapons justifies pursuing imperfect verification rather than no standard
existing international law has significant gaps for autonomous systems — the principle of distinction and proportionality require human judgment that autonomous systems cannot exercise
moratorium on fully autonomous lethal systems is more achievable and more clearly justified than moratorium on all autonomous defense systems
Judgment
on balance, a moratorium on fully autonomous lethal systems is defensible — it is achievable, clearly justified by existing international humanitarian law principles, and addresses the most serious risks
the stronger case leads with the human accountability argument — it is grounded in existing international law and does not require new principles, only new application
the defensible position on verification is that imperfect verification with consequences for violation is better than no standard — the same logic applies to all arms control agreements
the judgment on scope is that fully autonomous vs. human-supervised is the critical distinction — the moratorium should target systems that select and engage targets without human decision, not systems that assist human decision-makers
Q8 Should there be a global ban on nuclear weapons?
INT ▼
Tensions
existential risk elimination vs. deterrence framework collapse
moral prohibition argument vs. strategic stability argument
universal compliance requirement vs. partial adoption creating asymmetric vulnerability
verification feasibility vs. scale of harm if ban fails
Priorities
existential risk elimination is the strongest argument; deterrence framework collapse is the strongest objection — the question is whether alternative security frameworks can compensate
moral prohibition is foundational; strategic stability argument is empirically contested — deterrence has prevented major war but also come close to catastrophic failure multiple times
partial adoption creating asymmetric vulnerability is the strongest practical objection — a ban that major nuclear powers don't join leaves non-nuclear signatories at risk
verification is imperfect but the scale of harm from nuclear war justifies pursuing imperfect verification — the same logic applies here as for autonomous weapons
Judgment
on balance, universal nuclear disarmament is the right long-term goal but requires alternative security frameworks before it is achievable — the ban is defensible as aspiration and as incremental pressure
the stronger case leads with the near-miss argument — deterrence has prevented war but has also come closer to catastrophic failure than is commonly acknowledged
the defensible position on partial adoption is that the ban creates meaningful normative pressure even without universal compliance — the NPT demonstrates that partial frameworks have real effects
the judgment on verification is that it is a technical challenge that improves over time — the argument from imperfect verification is an argument for building better verification, not for abandoning the goal
