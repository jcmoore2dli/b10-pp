Q1 Should students with disabilities always be fully included in mainstream classrooms?
EDU ▼
Tensions
inclusion as a civil right vs. inclusion without support as a disservice to all students
social integration benefits vs. risk of inadequate specialist support in mainstream settings
legal mandates for inclusion vs. parental right to choose specialized placement
long-term normalization of disability vs. short-term instructional disruption
Priorities
civil rights framing is strong, but rights without resources produce hollow inclusion — support must accompany the mandate
social integration benefits are real and documented, but they depend on adequate specialist staffing
legal mandates appropriately set the floor; parental choice operates within that floor, not above it
long-term normalization is the more durable social good; short-term disruption is manageable with investment
Judgment
on balance, full inclusion with embedded specialist support is defensible — the civil rights argument holds when resources follow
the stronger case is conditional: inclusion is defensible when support is adequate and harmful when it is not
the defensible position honors the mandate while protecting the right to appropriate placement for students with complex needs
long-term normalization outweighs short-term disruption — but only if the investment in support is genuine, not rhetorical
Q2 Should companies be required to provide childcare for employees?
WRK ▼
Tensions
employer obligation to support parental workforce participation vs. undue burden on small businesses
gender equity benefit vs. unequal benefit perception among non-parent employees
government mandate vs. market competition as mechanism for childcare provision
workforce retention benefit to employers vs. compliance cost of uniform mandate
Priorities
employer obligation is strongest for large employers; small business burden requires proportional threshold design
gender equity benefit is structural and documented; non-parent perception is manageable through transparent communication
mandate is appropriate where market competition has demonstrably failed to produce adequate childcare supply
retention benefit accrues to employers — this is not purely a cost but a return on investment argument
Judgment
on balance, a mandate with employer size threshold is defensible — the equity benefit is real and the burden is addressable through design
the stronger case acknowledges the non-parent perception issue while noting that structural equity interventions routinely appear unequal in the short term
where markets have failed to produce adequate childcare, mandate is the appropriate corrective — the defensible position is conditional on market failure evidence
the retention argument shifts this from pure cost to return on investment — employers who resist the mandate may be undervaluing the benefit
Q3 Should countries adopt a global minimum corporate tax?
ECN ▼
Tensions
tax sovereignty of nations vs. collective action failure of race-to-the-bottom tax competition
revenue recovery for high-tax nations vs. economic model disruption for tax havens
enforcement feasibility vs. scale of profit-shifting harm
benefit to developed nations vs. risk of developing nation exclusion from gains
Priorities
sovereignty argument is legitimate but does not override the collective action failure — no single nation can solve race-to-the-bottom alone
revenue recovery is documented and substantial; tax haven disruption is real but concentrated in a small number of economies
enforcement is imperfect but improving — imperfect enforcement of a sound principle is preferable to no standard
developing nation inclusion must be a design requirement, not an afterthought — the case for the minimum tax weakens if its benefits are captured only by wealthy nations
Judgment
on balance, a global minimum corporate tax is defensible — the collective action failure argument is stronger than the sovereignty objection
the stronger case acknowledges tax haven disruption while noting that the disruption is concentrated and the benefit is diffuse
the defensible position on enforcement is that imperfect compliance is better than no standard — the direction of travel matters
the judgment depends on whether developing nations are included in the benefit design — if not, the equity case collapses
Q4 Should governments be required to consult citizens directly on major policy decisions?
GOV ▼
Tensions
democratic legitimacy requires citizen voice vs. representative democracy was designed to buffer uninformed majorities
consultation improves policy outcomes vs. consultation slows decision-making on urgent issues
majority preference vs. minority rights protection
citizen engagement value vs. manipulation of consultation by organized interests
Priorities
legitimacy argument is strong for high-stakes, low-urgency decisions; urgency argument wins for security and climate emergencies
consultation improves outcomes when participants are adequately informed — the quality of process determines whether the benefit materializes
majority preference is legitimate within constitutional constraints; minority rights require structural protection regardless of consultation design
manipulation risk is real but addressable through deliberative design — random citizen assemblies outperform open referenda on this dimension
Judgment
on balance, structured deliberative consultation is defensible for major non-urgent decisions — the legitimacy benefit is real when process quality is high
the stronger case is conditional: consultation improves outcomes when designed for informed deliberation, not when designed for maximum participation regardless of quality
the defensible position protects minority rights through constitutional constraints on what consultation can decide, not by eliminating consultation
the judgment on manipulation risk favors deliberative assemblies over open referenda — design determines whether the benefit or the risk dominates
Q5 Should mental health services be equally funded as physical health services?
HLT ▼
Tensions
parity as a matter of justice vs. resource allocation should follow evidence of impact
long-term cost savings from treatment vs. short-term fiscal cost of parity
workforce shortage constrains impact of increased funding vs. funding is prerequisite for workforce expansion
stigma reduction as downstream benefit vs. stigma as barrier to utilization even when access improves
Priorities
justice argument is strong and does not require purely utilitarian justification — but evidence of impact strengthens rather than replaces it
long-term cost savings are documented; short-term fiscal cost is real but one-time in character
the chicken-and-egg argument favors funding first — workforce expansion follows investment, not the other way around
stigma reduction is a downstream benefit that funding alone does not produce — public awareness investment must accompany service expansion
Judgment
on balance, mental health parity is defensible on both justice and utilitarian grounds — the arguments reinforce rather than compete with each other
the stronger case leads with the cost savings argument for fiscally skeptical audiences while retaining the justice argument as the foundational claim
the defensible position on workforce shortage is to fund expansion and training pipelines simultaneously — sequential logic does not apply
the judgment on stigma is that funding is necessary but not sufficient — parity legislation without public awareness investment underdelivers on its own goals
Q6 Should tech companies be required to pay taxes in every country they operate?
TEC ▼
Tensions
digital presence as equivalent to physical presence for tax purposes vs. existing treaty frameworks should govern
developing nation revenue access vs. double taxation risk for compliant companies
enforcement feasibility vs. scale of profit-shifting harm
consumer price impact of increased corporate tax vs. public revenue benefit
Priorities
digital presence argument is more compelling than treaty framework defense — treaties were designed for a physical economy
developing nation revenue access is the strongest equity argument; double taxation is addressable through careful design
enforcement is imperfect but improving; the scale of documented profit-shifting harm makes imperfect enforcement worth pursuing
consumer price impact is real but modest and distributable — public revenue benefit is substantial and concentrated in under-resourced governments
Judgment
on balance, requiring digital companies to pay taxes where they earn revenue is defensible — the treaty framework argument defends the status quo, not a principle
the stronger case leads with developing nation revenue access — it reframes the debate from corporate tax avoidance to global fiscal equity
the defensible position on enforcement is that coordinated imperfect enforcement is better than uncoordinated non-enforcement
the consumer price impact argument does not outweigh the public revenue benefit — the incidence is manageable and the benefit is substantial
Q7 Should countries be required to contribute to a global climate fund?
INT ▼
Tensions
historical emissions responsibility vs. current fiscal capacity as the basis for contribution
mandatory contributions vs. voluntary pledges as the more reliable mechanism
fund governance and transparency vs. political resistance in contributing nations
adaptation funding for vulnerable nations vs. mitigation funding for emissions reduction
Priorities
historical responsibility and current capacity are both legitimate bases — the strongest framework combines them proportionally
voluntary pledges have a documented record of underfulfillment — mandatory contributions with enforcement are more reliable
governance quality is a design requirement that determines whether the fund is worth defending — transparency must be built in from the start
adaptation and mitigation serve different populations — the most vulnerable nations need adaptation most urgently
Judgment
on balance, mandatory contributions with proportional formula are defensible — voluntary pledge history makes the case for mandate compelling
the stronger case combines historical responsibility with current capacity — neither alone produces a defensible contribution formula
the defensible position on governance is that transparency is non-negotiable — a poorly governed fund undermines the case for mandatory contributions
the judgment on adaptation vs. mitigation favors adaptation prioritization for the most climate-vulnerable nations — their survival is the most urgent claim on fund resources
Q8 Should governments fund the preservation of indigenous cultural practices?
GOV ▼
Tensions
historical dispossession creates ongoing obligation vs. cultural preservation is community responsibility
self-determination requires resources vs. government funding creates dependency
cultural diversity as public good vs. taxpayer resistance to funding specific group practices
language revitalization as prerequisite for cultural transmission vs. material cultural practices as more immediately fundable
Priorities
historical dispossession argument is strong and does not require purely utilitarian justification — but public good framing strengthens political viability
self-determination requires resources — the dependency argument is strongest when funding comes with strings attached, not when community governance controls the funds
cultural diversity as public good is the most defensible framing for general audiences — it shifts the question from special interest to shared benefit
language is the primary transmission medium for most cultural knowledge — funding material practices without funding language revitalization is incomplete
Judgment
on balance, government funding of indigenous cultural preservation is defensible when administered through community-controlled governance structures
the stronger case separates the funding question from the governance question — funding is justified by historical obligation; governance determines whether it serves self-determination or undermines it
the defensible framing is cultural diversity as public good, not special interest — it builds a broader coalition and reflects the genuine benefit
the judgment on language vs. material practices is that language revitalization should be the priority — it is the infrastructure of cultural transmission, not one element among many
