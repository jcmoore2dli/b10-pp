W5 — Values, Heuristics & Bias · ESO Phrase Reference

B10 Practice Platform · Tap a question to expand

About this frame: Identify the value framework that drives a stakeholder's position — what they believe is important, what they must assume to be true, and what a stakeholder on the other side believes instead. The analytical move is not stating your own opinion but surfacing the values and assumptions that make different positions coherent on their own terms.
Value The core value driving a stakeholder's position
Assumption What must be true for that value to justify the position
Competing Framework The value a different stakeholder holds, leading to a different conclusion
Key connectors: for those who prioritize… · from the perspective of… · this position assumes that… · those who hold this view believe that… · a stakeholder who values… would argue that… · the competing position rests on the assumption that… · while X stakeholders emphasize… those on the other side argue that… · this depends on whether one values… or…
Q1Should universities limit legacy admissions?
EDU▼
Value
merit as the basis for access to educational opportunity
equal opportunity requires dismantling inherited advantage
universities have a social equity obligation
first-generation students deserve a level playing field
Assumption
legacy preferences meaningfully reduce access for qualified non-legacy applicants
eliminating legacy will not significantly reduce alumni donation rates
admissions can be made merit-based without other structural biases remaining
universities' social obligations override their private institutional interests
Competing Framework
institutional sustainability depends on donor loyalty that legacy preference maintains
private institutions should retain autonomy over their own selection processes
family connection to an institution is a legitimate form of social capital
true equity requires addressing upstream preparation inequality, not just admissions criteria
Q2Should companies be required to disclose pay scales for all positions?
WRK▼
Value
pay equity as a right, not a negotiating outcome
transparency as a mechanism for structural discrimination reduction
workers' right to information about the value of their labor
gender and racial equity require systemic intervention, not individual negotiation
Assumption
pay transparency reduces discriminatory gaps rather than creating internal resentment
employees will use salary information to negotiate rather than simply feel demotivated
employers cannot effectively standardize compensation to game disclosure requirements
the benefit to underpaid workers outweighs disruption to existing compensation structures
Competing Framework
compensation is a private matter between employer and employee
market mechanisms and individual negotiation should determine pay outcomes
forced transparency undermines managerial flexibility and organizational culture
high performers deserve privacy around compensation that reflects their individual value
Q3Should inheritance taxes be significantly increased?
ECN▼
Value
equal opportunity requires interrupting intergenerational wealth concentration
accumulated wealth that was built with public infrastructure should contribute to public good
dynastic wealth is incompatible with a genuinely meritocratic society
fiscal capacity for public investment justifies redistribution at death
Assumption
increased inheritance tax meaningfully reduces wealth concentration rather than being avoided
wealth passed between generations primarily reflects luck rather than merit
tax revenue will be invested in genuine equal opportunity programs
work incentives are not significantly reduced by taxing wealth at death rather than during life
Competing Framework
the right to pass accumulated wealth to one's family is a fundamental property right
wealth was already taxed during accumulation — taxing it again at death is double taxation
family businesses and farms should not face forced sale to meet estate tax liability
individual economic freedom includes the freedom to determine the fate of one's own assets
Q4Should there be stricter limits on political donations from corporations?
GOV▼
Value
political equality — one person, one vote — requires limiting concentrated financial influence
democratic legitimacy depends on policy reflecting citizen rather than donor preferences
corporations are not citizens and should not have equivalent political voice
regulatory capture is a systemic democratic threat that donation limits help prevent
Assumption
corporate donations demonstrably shift legislative outcomes in donors' favor
limiting donations will not simply shift spending to harder-to-regulate channels
the Supreme Court's equation of money with speech can be legally challenged or narrowed
political equality is more important than freedom of political expenditure
Competing Framework
political spending is a form of protected free speech that government cannot limit
corporations represent the interests of shareholders and employees who have legitimate political stakes
market competition among political donors produces better policy outcomes than restriction
the same limits that restrict corporate donations also restrict union and civil society advocacy
Q5Should euthanasia be legalized in all countries?
HLT▼
Value
individual autonomy over one's own body extends to end-of-life decisions
dignity in dying is as important as dignity in living
relief from intractable suffering is a legitimate medical and moral goal
patient self-determination should be the governing principle of medical care
Assumption
safeguards can reliably prevent coercion of vulnerable patients
patients in terminal distress are capable of making fully autonomous decisions
the availability of euthanasia does not reduce investment in palliative care alternatives
legalizing euthanasia does not lead to expanding eligibility criteria over time
Competing Framework
life has inherent value that medical professionals are obligated to preserve, not end
the medical profession's duty of care is fundamentally incompatible with administering death
vulnerable populations — elderly, disabled, economically dependent — face systemic coercion risk
high-quality palliative care eliminates the suffering that drives euthanasia requests
Q6Should governments regulate the collection and use of biometric data?
TEC▼
Value
bodily integrity extends to biometric data — it is uniquely personal and irreplaceable
the state's surveillance capacity must be structurally limited to protect civil liberties
racial and demographic equity requires preventing biased algorithmic identification
citizens have a right to move through public space without being identified and tracked
Assumption
biometric surveillance infrastructure, once built, will expand beyond its original purpose
algorithmic bias in facial recognition produces demonstrably discriminatory outcomes
government regulation can effectively constrain private sector biometric collection
the convenience benefits of biometric authentication do not justify the privacy cost
Competing Framework
public safety and security justify proportionate use of biometric identification tools
biometric authentication provides security and convenience benefits that regulation would eliminate
law enforcement effectiveness in preventing crime justifies facial recognition deployment
citizens who have nothing to hide have nothing to fear from biometric identification
Q7Should private prisons be banned?
JUS▼
Value
justice is a public good that cannot be contracted to profit-seeking entities
incarcerated individuals retain rights that profit incentives structurally undermine
rehabilitation requires an institutional orientation that profit motive is incompatible with
the state's monopoly on legitimate use of force cannot be outsourced to private interests
Assumption
private prison profit motive demonstrably produces worse conditions and outcomes
government can manage correctional facilities more effectively than private operators
eliminating profit motive reduces systemic incentive to maintain high incarceration rates
public sector corrections will invest savings from profit elimination into rehabilitation
Competing Framework
market competition between public and private operators improves overall system efficiency
private operators can deliver equivalent outcomes at lower cost to taxpayers
rural communities that depend on prison employment have legitimate economic interests
government bureaucracies are no less prone to poor outcomes than private contractors
Q8Should there be stricter international regulations on the use of private military companies?
INT▼
Value
civilian protection in conflict requires human accountability that profit motive undermines
the state's monopoly on legitimate armed force is a foundational principle of international order
those who cause harm in conflict must be legally accountable regardless of employment status
democratic oversight of military action requires transparency that PMC use circumvents
Assumption
international regulations can be enforced meaningfully against PMCs operating across jurisdictions
states will comply with regulations that limit their ability to use PMCs for deniable operations
PMC accountability can be achieved without eliminating their legitimate security functions
existing international humanitarian law can be extended to cover non-state armed actors
Competing Framework
states need flexible security capacity that PMCs provide without the political cost of troop deployment
PMCs fill legitimate security gaps that state militaries cannot or will not address
over-regulation drives PMC activity to less accountable jurisdictions rather than eliminating it
national security interests justify forms of military flexibility that strict regulation would eliminate

How to Use These Phrases

Tap a question to expand its three columns. Unlike the other frames, these three columns are parallel, not sequential — they don't chain into each other. Use them to construct an argument that surfaces the values and assumptions driving different stakeholder positions. For example:

"For those who prioritize individual autonomy over end-of-life decisions, euthanasia legalization is defensible — but this position assumes that safeguards can reliably prevent coercion of vulnerable patients. Those operating from a competing framework argue that the medical profession's duty of care is fundamentally incompatible with administering death, and that high-quality palliative care eliminates the suffering that drives euthanasia requests in the first place."

You are not stating your own values. You are identifying the value frameworks that make each stakeholder's position coherent on its own terms — and then showing that the disagreement is not primarily empirical but normative.

Scaffold feedback is diagnostic only — your score is always holistic.
