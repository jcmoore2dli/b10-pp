Week 6 Enabling Activities

Synthesis & Judgment
This set covers the full six-activity family for Synthesis & Judgment, organized as two tracks: 1a–1c build the Tensions → Priorities → Judgment move up in stages (depth ladder, sentence-frame progression, comparison & paraphrase); 4a–4c test whether it has actually proceduralized (2-box induction, silent protocol, strategic competence under vocabulary constraint). Because W6 is the platform's spiral week, every activity below also requires integrating at least one prior frame (W1–W5) — that requirement is folded into each activity's content rather than treated as a separate task. These supplement — they do not replace — the Week 6 folder and the ESO/Frames Daily Protocol.
This Week's Frame — Synthesis & Judgment (W6)
TensionsWhat competing claims must be addressed?
PrioritiesWhich consideration carries more weight, and why?
JudgmentWhat is the most defensible conclusion?
Key requirement: every response this week integrates at least one prior frame (W1–W5) as part of naming the tensions or defending the priority — Synthesis & Judgment doesn't introduce new analytical content, it draws on and weighs frames already practiced. A response that names a tension but never commits to a priority is operating at ILR 2+, not ILR 3 — describing disagreement is not the same as resolving it. The judgment must name which priority wins and why.

Activity Index

1a. Single-Scenario Depth Ladder  ·  1b. Sentence-Frame Progression  ·  1c. 3-Interval Comparison & Paraphrase  ·  4a. 2-Box Induction  ·  4b. Silent Protocol & Brief-Back  ·  4c. Strategic Competence & Fallback
Activity 1a · Depth Ladder
Single-Scenario Depth Ladder — "Scaffold the Scaffold"
Holds one low-stakes scenario constant while building the Tensions → Priorities → Judgment move in four steps, isolating exactly one new demand per step. Item 2 adds priority-setting alone; Item 3 adds the spiral defense — a prior frame's move used to justify that priority — together with the judgment itself, since defending a weighting and reaching a conclusion are the two hardest, most ILR-3-specific demands of this frame and shouldn't be asked for before the easier groundwork is secure. Item 4 removes all support and doesn't name which prior frame to use, since recognizing that is itself part of the transfer demand at full strength.
Anchor Scenario (held constant across Items 1–3)
A country is deciding whether to adopt a universal basic income (UBI).
1
Surface "What are the two competing claims in the UBI debate?"
Name one tension only — one value pulling each way (for example, security for low-income families versus cost to taxpayers).
2
Surface → Deep "Which consideration should carry more weight here?"
Add the Priorities box. State which one wins — the defense comes next, not yet.
3
Deep "If reducing poverty is weighted more heavily than short-term cost, the most defensible conclusion is that a country ___. Defend that weighting using a trade-off: what's given up either way, and why is one loss more tolerable than the other?"
Complete the Judgment and defend it with a prior-frame move in the same response — the two hardest demands of this frame, combined now that the tension and priority are already secure.
4
Transfer "Should countries replace their current retirement age with a flexible retirement window based on job type?"
Full response: tensions + priorities + judgment, integrating at least one prior frame of your choosing, no connector given.
Instructor note: this is the hardest week's ladder — expect it to take longer than 5–10 minutes. If a student stalls at Item 3, that's the diagnostic moment: they likely need more practice with a specific prior frame (probably W2 Trade-offs) before the spiral defense comes easily. A student who completes Item 4 by selecting and integrating their own prior frame, without being told which one, is ready for the Week 6 folder.
Activity 1b · Sentence-Frame Progression
Causal Subordination → Concessive Subordination → Appositive
Builds subordinate-clause complexity in three tiers using connectors that read as ILR 2+/3, not just connectors that are technically subordinating. Coordinating conjunctions (FANBOYS) are excluded — and so are "because" and "so." Unlike every other week, T2 and T3 here can't just hold a tension — per this week's resolved design, they have to name the tension and resolve it toward one side using a prior frame's reasoning, in the same sentence.
Scenario
A country is deciding whether to adopt a universal basic income (UBI).
T1
Causal subordination — given that / on the grounds that / considering that Complete each sentence, naming one priority — no resolution required yet.
  • "UBI advocates prioritize poverty reduction given that ___."
  • "Skeptics remain cautious about the cost on the grounds that ___."
  • "Some economists support a pilot program considering that ___."
T2
Concessive subordination — although / while / even though Now name the tension and resolve it in the same sentence — harder than T1 because the clause has to concede the losing side's point before justifying why it still loses.
  • "While critics worry about UBI's cost to taxpayers, that cost is more tolerable than continued poverty because ___."
  • "Although a universal payment reaches people who don't strictly need it, the simplicity is worth that inefficiency given that ___."
  • "Even though the program requires new taxes, the burden is justified rather than the alternative because ___."
T3a
Restrictive relative clauses — "Fact who ___, Fact" Complete each sentence with a relative clause. Then combine them into one sentence using a subordinating conjunction of your choice.
  • "Advocates who ___ argue UBI eliminates poverty traps in the current system."
  • "Skeptics who ___ question whether broad payments are fiscally sustainable."
T3b
Appositives —
Appositives — "Fact, ___, Fact" Embed a prior-frame-grounded justification as a defining phrase inside the sentence.
Model
"The cost to taxpayers, a burden that critics call disproportionate at the organizational level, is outweighed by the security UBI provides to individual families living paycheck to paycheck — the same scale-based trade-off that makes redistribution defensible in most safety-net debates."
Now write your own appositive sentence that names a tension, resolves it toward one side, and grounds that resolution in a prior frame's reasoning.
Instructor note: "because" and "so" aren't FANBOYS, but they're exactly the safe, overused connectors proficiency training flags as a ceiling. T1 swaps them for "given that" / "on the grounds that" / "considering that" — same causal-reasoning entry point, higher register from the first sentence.
Instructor note: move to T2 only once T1 is fluent. Unlike other weeks, T3 here isn't optional stretch — it's the clearest single-sentence test of whether resolution-plus-integration has landed.
Activity 1c · 3-Interval Comparison
Comparison & Paraphrase Prompts
Connects synthesis vocabulary to comparative sentence structures, moving from a simple one-line comparison to a claim that resolves the comparison and names the prior frame doing the work.
Comparison Pair
How the security UBI provides to individuals compares with its cost to the broader tax base — and which consideration should win.
Target Lexicon — must appear at least once
outweighsdefensibleresolves in light ofon balance
1
Surface "In one sentence, compare the security UBI provides to individuals with its cost to the broader tax base."
2
Deep "Using defensible, explain which side of that comparison is stronger and why."
3
Transfer "Write one complex sentence that resolves the comparison — using outweighs or on balance — and explicitly names which prior frame (W1–W5) justifies your resolution."
Instructor note: score for lexicon use and whether the student explicitly names which prior frame justifies the resolution — not for which side they land on.
Activity 4a · 2-Box Induction
Visual Synthesis
Forces students to distinguish a resolved judgment from a restated tension — and to see the spiral requirement modeled in context — from grouped, unlabeled examples. No rule is stated up front.
Shared Facts (both boxes work from this)
A city is debating whether to close a beloved but financially struggling public swimming pool to save money, or keep it open by raising local taxes.
Box A
  • There are good reasons to keep the pool open and good reasons to close it. Both sides have valid points, and the city needs to weigh cost against community value.
  • Ultimately, this is a difficult decision that depends on what the city values most.
Box B
  • The pool should stay open. At the individual and family level, it's one of the only affordable recreational spaces for lower-income residents — losing it would fall hardest on exactly the households least able to absorb the loss.
  • At the organizational level, the tax increase needed is small relative to the whole city budget. Because the burden of closing falls disproportionately on one group while the burden of the tax is spread broadly, keeping the pool open is the more defensible choice.
Task
What does Box B do that Box A never does? Why isn't "both sides have good points" a judgment, even though it sounds balanced and reasonable? Which prior frame is Box B drawing on to defend its priority?
Instructor note: don't pre-explain the boxes. Box A sounds thoughtful but never commits to a priority or a conclusion — it's a restated tension wearing the shape of a judgment, exactly the ILR 2+ ceiling this frame is designed to push past. Box B commits, and defends the priority using a W1-style scale/stakeholder move (individual/family level versus organizational level). Let the student name both things before you confirm them.
Activity 4b · Silent Protocol
Silent Modeling & Pre-Attempt Brief-Back
Separates rule comprehension from production. Unlike every other week's silent model, this one marks four moves, not three — the fourth being which prior frame is doing the work, since for W6 that's not incidental, it's the thing being tested.
Phase 1 — Silent Modeling
Read the response below. Four colors mark four different moves. No explanation is given — figure out what each color represents before moving to Phase 2.
Critics argue UBI is too costly, while advocates insist the security it provides justifies the expense. Between those two claims, the security argument carries more weight, because the cost of doing nothing — persistent poverty and its downstream strain on emergency services, healthcare, and lost productivity — is higher than the cost of the program itself. Weighing the individual-level benefit of guaranteed income against the organizational-level tax burden, the individual security outweighs the aggregate cost, since the burden is spread thinly across many taxpayers while the benefit is concentrated where it's needed most.
Key (reveal only after Phase 2):
Tension Priority Prior Frame Used (W1) Judgment
Phase 2 — Verbal Brief-Back ("Plan Before You Produce")
Before attempting your own response to the prompt below, say out loud — or write in one sentence — what tension you'll name, which priority wins, and which prior frame you'll use to defend that priority. Do not begin your full response until you've stated your plan.
Prompt for Phase 2 Attempt
"Should a universal basic income replace unemployment benefits entirely, or supplement them?"
Instructor note: the brief-back is a planning checkpoint, not a rehearsal — keep it to one sentence. This models a legitimate real-world strategy: a brief pause to organize before speaking.
Activity 4c · Strategic Competence
Fallback Scaffolding
Tests whether the tensions-priorities-judgment move survives without the frame's own vocabulary — and, for this week only, without naming the prior frame being used either. The reasoning has to be there; neither label does. This is the ILR 3 skill in its purest form for this platform: fluent synthesis with no scaffolding language left to lean on.
Constraint
Restate the argument below without using the bolded words. Add hedging language as you restate.
Argument to Restate
"The UBI debate surfaces a genuine tension: individual security versus collective fiscal cost. The priority that carries more weight is security — because the cost of persistent poverty, measured in emergency services, healthcare strain, and lost productivity, exceeds the cost of the program itself. The most defensible judgment is that a modest UBI, funded through progressive taxation, produces a net social benefit when the full accounting is done."
Prompt
"Should countries replace their current retirement age with a flexible retirement window based on job type?"
Fallback Strategies to Practice
a
Circumlocution Talk around the missing word — instead of "my judgment is," try "what makes the most sense here is" or "the strongest conclusion is."
b
Term substitution Instead of naming a prior frame directly ("there's a trade-off between X and Y"), just do the reasoning without labeling it: "gaining X means giving up Y, and that's worth it because..."
c
Self-correction "What I mean is..." / "Let me put that another way..."
d
Hedging "It seems like the strongest case is..." / "This tends to matter more when..."
Instructor note: use only after a student is reliably integrating a prior frame by name in Activities 1a–4b. This activity tests whether the reasoning survives once the scaffolding language disappears entirely — a stress test, not a first attempt.
