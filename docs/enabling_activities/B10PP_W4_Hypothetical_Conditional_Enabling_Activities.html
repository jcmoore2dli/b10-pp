Week 4 Enabling Activities

Hypothetical & Conditional
This set covers the full six-activity family for Hypothetical & Conditional, organized as two tracks: 1a–1c build the Condition → Projected Outcome → Extended Scenario move up in stages (depth ladder, sentence-frame progression, comparison & paraphrase); 4a–4c test whether it has actually proceduralized (2-box induction, silent protocol, strategic competence under vocabulary constraint). These supplement — they do not replace — the Week 4 folder and the ESO/Frames Daily Protocol.
This Week's Frame — Hypothetical & Conditional (W4)
ConditionWhat condition must hold for this scenario to follow?
Projected OutcomeWhat would likely result?
Extended ScenarioAnd if that outcome holds, then what?
Key requirement: the extended scenario must go one step beyond the projected outcome. A response that states the condition and the immediate result but stops there is only two-thirds of the move — the frame requires projecting what happens next, after that first outcome takes hold.

Activity 1a · Depth Ladder
Single-Scenario Depth Ladder — "Scaffold the Scaffold"
Holds one low-stakes scenario constant while building the Condition → Projected Outcome → Extended Scenario move in four steps, so the mechanics of the frame — not an unfamiliar topic — are what's being tested. Use before a student's first W4 attempt, or as remediation if responses stop at the first outcome.
Anchor Scenario (held constant across all four items)
A country lowers its voting age to 16.
1
Surface "If a country lowers its voting age to 16, what is the condition, exactly?"
State the condition precisely — what has to actually be true.
2
Surface → Deep "What would likely result immediately from that condition?"
Add the Projected Outcome box. First-order result only — stop here for now.
3
Deep "If the voting age drops to 16, more young people would likely vote in that first election. And if that higher turnout holds over several elections, then ___."
Complete the Extended Scenario — the second-order consequence, one step further than the first outcome.
4
Transfer "What would happen if countries made voting mandatory for all adults?"
Full response: condition + projected outcome + extended scenario, no connector given.
Instructor note: 5–10 minute warm-up. A student who completes Item 4 without hesitation is ready for the Week 4 folder.
Activity 1b · Sentence-Frame Progression
Causal Subordination → Concessive Subordination → Appositive
Builds subordinate-clause complexity in three tiers using connectors that read as ILR 2+/3, not just connectors that are technically subordinating. Coordinating conjunctions (FANBOYS) are excluded — and so are "because" and "so": both are grammatically fine, but they're exactly the safe, overused connectors proficiency training pushes students away from. T1 uses higher-register causal subordinators that do the same causal job with more precision.
Scenario
A country lowers its voting age to 16.
T1
Causal subordination — given that / on the grounds that / considering that Complete each sentence, naming one condition-to-outcome link.
  • "Turnout among young voters would likely rise given that ___."
  • "Election officials are willing to lower the voting age on the grounds that ___."
  • "Some critics remain skeptical of the change considering that ___."
T2
Concessive subordination — although / while / even though Now hold the immediate outcome and the extended scenario in tension inside one sentence — harder than T1 because the clause has to concede that a modest first result doesn't rule out a larger effect later.
  • "While younger voters might turn out modestly in the first election, the long-term effect ___."
  • "Although the immediate outcome seems small, the extended scenario ___."
  • "Even though critics doubt any short-term impact, sustained turnout over several elections ___."
T3
Appositives — "Fact, ___, Fact" Embed a condition's or scenario's role in the projection as a defining phrase inside the sentence.
Model
"The lowered voting age, the initial condition that opens the door to younger participation, produces a modest bump in first-time turnout, while the extended pattern of civic habit-forming, compounding across several election cycles, eventually reshapes which issues campaigns prioritize."
Now write your own appositive sentence connecting a condition to its extended, second-order scenario.
Instructor note: "because" and "so" aren't FANBOYS, but they're exactly the safe, overused connectors proficiency training flags as a ceiling. T1 swaps them for "given that" / "on the grounds that" / "considering that" — same causal-reasoning entry point (still vital groundwork for projecting an outcome), higher register from the first sentence.
Instructor note: move to T2 only once T1 is fluent. T3 is stretch — optional for students still consolidating the one-step-further requirement.
Activity 1c · 3-Interval Comparison
Comparison & Paraphrase Prompts
Connects conditional-projection vocabulary to comparative sentence structures, moving from a simple one-line comparison to a claim that explicitly extends one step past the first outcome.
Comparison Pair
How the voting age policy's first-election outcome compares with its extended effect across several election cycles.
Target Lexicon — must appear at least once
contingent onwere [X] to occurin turn absent [X]downstream
1
Surface "In one sentence, compare the immediate outcome of lowering the voting age with its extended effect over several elections."
2
Deep "Using contingent on, explain what the extended scenario depends on beyond the first election's turnout."
3
Transfer "Write one complex sentence connecting the projected outcome to the extended scenario, using in turn or downstream."
Instructor note: score for lexicon use and whether the extended scenario genuinely goes one step beyond the first outcome — not for stance on lowering the voting age.
Activity 4a · 2-Box Induction
Visual Synthesis
Forces students to notice when a conditional claim's condition has gone unstated from grouped, unlabeled examples — no rule is stated up front.
Box A
  • If the city extends the subway line into the suburbs, downtown traffic would likely ease during rush hour.
  • If schools eliminate standardized testing, teachers would likely have more flexibility in lesson planning.
  • If the company adopts a four-day week, employee retention would likely improve.
Box B
  • Downtown traffic would likely ease during rush hour.
  • Teachers would likely have more flexibility in lesson planning.
  • Employee retention would likely improve.
Task
Box B's claims could just as easily be simple predictions about the future — nothing marks them as conditional reasoning. What is missing from Box B that Box A has? Why does naming the condition explicitly matter for this week's frame, even when the outcome itself is easy to guess?
Instructor note: don't pre-explain the boxes. This is a common failure mode at ILR 2+: students describe a plausible future without ever grounding it in the "if" that makes it conditional reasoning rather than a guess. Let the student name what's missing before you confirm it.
Activity 4b · Silent Protocol
Silent Modeling & Pre-Attempt Brief-Back
Separates rule comprehension from production. Students extract the frame's structure from a marked-but-unexplained model, then must plan out loud before they're allowed to attempt their own response.
Phase 1 — Silent Modeling
Read the response below. Three colors mark three different moves. No explanation is given — figure out what each color represents before moving to Phase 2.
If the country lowers its voting age to sixteen, a change that would immediately add several million newly eligible voters to the rolls before the next election, turnout among that age group would likely be modest at first, since first-time voters historically participate at lower rates than experienced ones regardless of age. But if those sixteen-year-olds vote even once before adulthood, research on civic habit-forming suggests they become measurably more likely to vote for the rest of their lives — meaning the policy's real effect wouldn't show up in the first election at all, but in the turnout data twenty years later.
Key (reveal only after Phase 2):
Condition Projected Outcome Extended Scenario
Phase 2 — Verbal Brief-Back ("Plan Before You Produce")
Before attempting your own response to the prompt below, say out loud — or write in one sentence — what condition you're assuming, what immediate outcome you expect, and what extended scenario follows from it. Do not begin your full response until you've stated your plan.
Prompt for Phase 2 Attempt
"What would happen if the voting age were lowered to sixteen only for local city elections, not national ones?"
Instructor note: the brief-back is a planning checkpoint, not a rehearsal — keep it to one sentence. This models a legitimate real-world strategy: a brief pause to organize before speaking.
Activity 4c · Strategic Competence
Fallback Scaffolding
Tests whether the condition-outcome-extended-scenario move survives without the frame's own vocabulary — the ILR 3 skill of knowing what to do when the exact word isn't available.
Constraint
Answer the prompt below without using the words condition, projected outcome, or extended scenario.
Prompt
"What would happen if countries made voting mandatory for all adults?"
Fallback Strategies to Practice
a
Circumlocution Talk around the missing word — instead of "condition," try "what has to be true first" or "the starting point."
b
Term substitution Instead of "projected outcome" / "extended scenario," try "what happens right away" / "what happens after that, over time."
c
Self-correction "What I mean is..." / "Let me put that another way..."
d
Hedging "It seems likely that..." / "This could eventually lead to..."
Instructor note: use after a student is reliably projecting one step beyond the first outcome with the frame vocabulary available — this is a stress test, not a first attempt.
