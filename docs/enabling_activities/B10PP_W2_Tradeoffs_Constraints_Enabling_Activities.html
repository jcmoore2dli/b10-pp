Week 2 Enabling Activities

Trade-offs & Constraints
This set covers the full six-activity family for Trade-offs & Constraints, organized as two tracks: 1a–1c build the Benefit → Trade-off → Constraint move up in stages (depth ladder, sentence-frame progression, comparison & paraphrase); 4a–4c test whether it has actually proceduralized (2-box induction, silent protocol, strategic competence under vocabulary constraint). These supplement — they do not replace — the Week 2 folder and the ESO/Frames Daily Protocol.
This Week's Frame — Trade-offs & Constraints (W2)
BenefitWhat does the policy achieve?
Trade-offWhat competing good is sacrificed?
ConstraintWhy can't the trade-off simply be avoided?
Key requirement: the constraint step must show why the trade-off is structural — a response that names what's given up without explaining why it can't be preserved alongside the benefit does not meet the standard for this frame. Naming a trade-off is not the same as explaining a constraint.

Activity 1a · Depth Ladder
Single-Scenario Depth Ladder — "Scaffold the Scaffold"
Holds one low-stakes scenario constant while building the Benefit → Trade-off → Constraint move in four steps, so the mechanics of the frame — not an unfamiliar topic — are what's being tested. Use before a student's first W2 attempt, or as remediation if the constraint step isn't yet showing up as structural.
Anchor Scenario (held constant across all four items)
A city converts a lane of street parking into a protected bike lane.
1
Surface "What does converting the parking lane to a bike lane achieve?"
Name one benefit only.
2
Surface → Deep "What is given up to get that benefit?"
Add the Trade-off box. Just name what's sacrificed — don't explain why yet.
3
Deep "The city gains safer cycling routes. Local businesses lose customer parking, and ___."
Complete the Constraint — why the trade-off can't just be undone (a second lane can't be added without removing something else; the street width is fixed).
4
Transfer "Should cities convert more downtown streets to bike lanes?"
Full response: benefit + trade-off + constraint, no connector given.
Instructor note: 5–10 minute warm-up. A student who completes Item 4 without hesitation is ready for the Week 2 folder.
Activity 1b · Sentence-Frame Progression
Causal Subordination → Concessive Subordination → Appositive
Builds subordinate-clause complexity in three tiers using connectors that read as ILR 2+/3, not just connectors that are technically subordinating. Coordinating conjunctions (FANBOYS) are excluded — and so are "because" and "so": both are grammatically fine, but they're exactly the safe, overused connectors proficiency training pushes students away from. T1 uses higher-register causal subordinators that do the same causal job with more precision.
Scenario
A city converts a lane of street parking into a protected bike lane.
T1
Causal subordination — given that / on the grounds that / considering that Complete each sentence, naming one benefit.
  • "Cyclists support the new bike lane given that ___."
  • "The city council is willing to approve the conversion on the grounds that ___."
  • "Nearby businesses remain open to the idea considering that ___."
T2
Concessive subordination — although / while / even though Now hold the benefit and the trade-off in tension inside one sentence — harder than T1 because the clause has to concede a cost rather than simply state a reason.
  • "While cyclists gain a safer route, local drivers ___."
  • "Although the bike lane reduces accidents, nearby shop owners ___."
  • "Even though the city gains a greener transportation image, businesses along the route ___."
T3
Appositives — "Fact, ___, Fact" Embed a stakeholder's benefit-or-cost identity as a defining phrase inside the sentence.
Model
"Cyclists, the primary beneficiaries of the new protected lane, report feeling safer commuting, while local shop owners, facing a steep drop in curbside customers, argue the trade-off wasn't fairly weighed."
Now write your own appositive sentence connecting a benefit-holding stakeholder to the cost another stakeholder bears.
Instructor note: "because" and "so" aren't FANBOYS, but they're exactly the safe, overused connectors proficiency training flags as a ceiling. T1 swaps them for "given that" / "on the grounds that" / "considering that" — same causal-reasoning entry point (still vital groundwork for the Benefit → Trade-off move), higher register from the first sentence.
Instructor note: move to T2 only once T1 is fluent. T3 is stretch — optional for students still consolidating the two-sided trade-off requirement.
Activity 1c · 3-Interval Comparison
Comparison & Paraphrase Prompts
Connects trade-off vocabulary to comparative sentence structures, moving from a simple one-line comparison to a claim that explicitly names the trade-off as structural.
Comparison Pair
How the bike lane's benefit to cyclists compares with its cost to local businesses.
Target Lexicon — must appear at least once
trade-offinherentstructural at the expense ofon the whole
1
Surface "In one sentence, compare the benefit cyclists get from the new lane with the cost local businesses bear."
2
Deep "Using structural, explain whether the cost to businesses is incidental or built into the design of the bike lane itself."
3
Transfer "Write one complex sentence connecting the benefit to cyclists and the cost to businesses, using inherent or at the expense of."
Instructor note: score for lexicon use and whether the response correctly identifies the trade-off as structural — not for stance on whether the bike lane is worth it.
Activity 4a · 2-Box Induction
Visual Synthesis
Forces students to infer what makes a trade-off complete versus incomplete from grouped, unlabeled examples — no rule is stated up front.
Box A
  • The company saved $2 million a year by switching to remote work.
  • The city cut traffic accidents by installing more streetlights.
  • The school raised test scores by extending the school day.
Box B
  • The company saved $2 million a year by switching to remote work, but lost the spontaneous collaboration that used to happen in the office.
  • The city cut traffic accidents by installing more streetlights, but raised its annual energy costs by 15%.
  • The school raised test scores by extending the school day, but cut into students' time for after-school jobs and family responsibilities.
Task
What is missing from the statements in Box A that Box B includes? Why is a Box B–style statement a stronger analytical claim than a Box A–style statement? Answer using a concessive subordinating conjunction — although, while, or even though.
Instructor note: don't pre-explain the boxes. The distinction is presence versus absence of the trade-off — Box A statements aren't wrong, they're incomplete for this week's purposes. Let the student name what's missing before you confirm it.
Activity 4b · Silent Protocol
Silent Modeling & Pre-Attempt Brief-Back
Separates rule comprehension from production. Students extract the frame's structure from a marked-but-unexplained model, then must plan out loud before they're allowed to attempt their own response.
Phase 1 — Silent Modeling
Read the response below. Three colors mark three different moves. No explanation is given — figure out what each color represents before moving to Phase 2.
The city gains safer cycling routes and reduced traffic emissions along the corridor — a direct benefit for commuters who switch from driving to biking. But that same redesign removes forty on-street parking spaces that local shop owners relied on for customer turnover. Because the street's width is fixed, there's no way to add the bike lane without removing an equivalent amount of parking somewhere on that block — the trade-off isn't a side effect of poor planning, it's the only way the two uses can physically coexist on the same strip of pavement.
Key (reveal only after Phase 2):
Benefit Trade-off Constraint
Phase 2 — Verbal Brief-Back ("Plan Before You Produce")
Before attempting your own response to the prompt below, say out loud — or write in one sentence — what benefit and what trade-off you will name, and why you think the constraint makes the trade-off unavoidable. Do not begin your full response until you've stated your plan.
Prompt for Phase 2 Attempt
"Should cities make new bike lanes permanent after a trial period, or restore the parking once testing ends?"
Instructor note: the brief-back is a planning checkpoint, not a rehearsal — keep it to one sentence. This models a legitimate real-world strategy: a brief pause to organize before speaking.
Activity 4c · Strategic Competence
Fallback Scaffolding
Tests whether the benefit-trade-off-constraint move survives without the frame's own vocabulary — the ILR 3 skill of knowing what to do when the exact word isn't available.
Constraint
Answer the prompt below without using the words benefit, trade-off, or constraint.
Prompt
"Should cities convert more downtown streets to bike lanes?"
Fallback Strategies to Practice
a
Circumlocution Talk around the missing word — instead of "trade-off," try "what has to give" or "the thing you lose to get this."
b
Term substitution Instead of "benefit" / "constraint," try "what's gained" / "the reason it can't be avoided."
c
Self-correction "What I mean is..." / "Let me put that another way..."
d
Hedging "It seems to come with a cost..." / "This tends to require giving something up..."
Instructor note: use after a student is reliably naming the constraint as structural with the frame vocabulary available — this is a stress test, not a first attempt.
